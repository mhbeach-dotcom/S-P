The 11 course themes (guiding questions)
1. The Interrelationship of Policy, Strategy, and Operations

What were the most important political interests and objectives of the antagonists? How did these interests and objectives originate? What value did each participant in each case study place on its political objectives? Were these interests and objectives clearly articulated and understood? Were short-term, medium-term, and long-term objectives compatible or in conflict? Were the problems that gave rise to war susceptible to military resolution? If leaders decided to employ armed force in pursuit of political objectives, how did they plan to use other instruments of power in support of their strategy? Were these plans appropriate? Were costs and risks anticipated and commensurate with benefits and rewards?

What strategic guidance did political leaders provide the military, and what restraints did they impose? How did guidance and restraints impede or promote operational success? What strategies did the belligerents adopt? Did their strategies strike an appropriate balance between defense and offense? To what extent did strategies support their respective policies? At any point, did strategy drive policy? What assumptions did statesmen and military leaders make about the contribution of military objectives to attaining overarching political objectives? Was the outcome more the product of sound strategy and superior leadership on the part of the victors or of self-defeating courses of action by the losing side?

2. The Decision for War

What were the short-term and long-term causes of the war? What were the impediments to deterrence or compromise? Were better deterrent or appeasement strategies available? Why did deterrence fail?

Was the decision to go to war rational? Was the choice for war based on accurate assessment of one’s own capabilities, military potential, and vulnerabilities as well as those of the enemy? What role, if any, did military leaders play in the decision for war? Did they offer political leadership an analysis of the available strategic options? How did political objectives shape the decision for war? If war was preemptive or preventive, how accurate was the information about enemy action or potential? Was the outbreak of the war optimally timed from the standpoint of the belligerent that initiated it? To what extent did predictions about the behavior of coalition partners and neutral states factor into the decision for war? If the war began with a surprise attack, what impact did that attack have? If the decision for war involved an intervention in an ongoing conflict, was that intervention decisive?

How did race, ethnicity, religion, and ideology affect decisions? Did ambition, concern for reputation, historical analogies, or arrogance affect the decision-makers? Were peaceful strategies, potentially as promising or more promising than military ones, dismissed or overlooked? Did a third party drag major powers into a war that none of them wanted? Did one power miscalculate how another would respond to an aggressive or threatening action?

3. Intelligence, Assessment, and Plans

How reliable and complete was intelligence on the interests, intentions, capabilities, and will of rivals and potential enemies? Did politics or culture affect the gathering of intelligence? If a belligerent suffered a surprise attack, why was it taken by surprise? How successful were each belligerent’s efforts to shape perceptions of its capabilities and intentions? How well did each side assess its own and the enemy’s strengths and weaknesses? To what extent did civilian and military leaders correctly understand the nature of the war upon which they were embarking? How well did each belligerent understand the cultural values, religious practices, political system, military traditions, and military potential of its enemy? How was that understanding reflected in war plans? Were plans based on assumptions of racial superiority over the enemy? Did planners objectively evaluate the enemy’s capabilities, or were their assessments distorted by ethnic or racial bias?

What planning process did each belligerent have? What kind of mechanisms did each have to integrate non-military instruments of power? To what extent did the planners think about strategic issues, not simply operational concerns? How did planners prioritize theaters and fronts? If allies were included in the planning process, how did their participation modify war plans? Was a serious effort made to study previous wars, and if so, how did it affect planning? Did plans bear the imprint of service doctrines or reflect accepted principles of war? Did plans identify the enemy’s strategic center(s) of gravity or critical vulnerabilities? To what extent did plans rely upon deception, surprise, information operations, or psychological operations? What were the strategic effects planners sought to achieve? Did planning allow for the fog, friction, uncertainty, and chance of war? If a war of attrition was likely, did planners anticipate the stages through which such a war might pass and the full range of operations that might be necessary? Did the initial plans consider how and when the war would be terminated and what the nature of postwar peace would be?

4. The Instruments of National Power

Did political and military leaders understand the strategic capabilities, effects, and limitations of the forms of national power at their disposal? Did leaders consider the political, financial, social, and logistical constraints on the employment of national power? How well were diplomacy, economic initiatives, and information operations coordinated with military operations?

How well did diplomacy support military power? How well did military power support diplomacy? Did diplomats manage escalation to negotiate a timely and advantageous settlement? How well were economic resources used in support of political aims? If one belligerent engaged in economic warfare, how accurate were its assumptions about the effects of economic levers on the enemy? What role did other instruments of national power play in economic warfare? Did leaders develop an effective information campaign to reach multiple audiences? Were those information campaigns based on a sound understanding of the culture and society of their targets? How well did political and military leaders engage in strategic communication with their domestic audiences? How persuasive were the justifications for war and for the strategies to fight it?

Did military leadership integrate different forms of power for maximum strategic effectiveness? What limitations prevented optimal integration of land, naval, air, space, and cyber operations? Did military leaders understand the capabilities and limitations of their own and other branches of their armed forces? Did strategists exploit opportunities created by technological innovation? Did civilian and military leaders apply realistic intelligence estimates to analyze possible courses of action? How did technological change affect strategic results? Did a belligerent make effective use of unconventional or irregular warfare?

5. Interaction, Adaptation, and Reassessment

How accurately did belligerents foresee the consequences of interaction with their enemies? Did reassessment reveal flawed assumptions of earlier assessments? Did reassessment reconsider the enemy’s capabilities after the initial interaction? Did the existence of weapons of mass destruction influence that interaction? How did interaction alter initial strategies? Was one side able to make its adversary fight on its own preferred terms? How well did strategists and commanders adapt to what the enemy did? If war became an attritional conflict, how successful were the belligerents in intensifying the effects of attrition upon their opponents? Was the side that began on the defensive able to make a successful transition to the offensive? In opening or contesting a new theater, did the belligerent do so to continue a preexisting strategy, to overcome a stalemate in the original theater, to implement a new strategy to achieve a new policy objective, or to seize a new opportunity? Did it involve fighting the enemy in a different location or fighting an entirely new enemy? Did it make strategic sense to open or contest the new theater? Did the environment in the new theater favor operational success? How did the new theater influence the larger war? Did it make strategic sense to close a theater? What role did maritime power play in opening the theater, supporting operations, and closing the theater?

If initial strategies proved successful, did that strategic success drive changes, whether wise or foolish, in political objectives? If initial strategies proved unsuccessful or too costly, was there a reassessment of political objectives, strategy, or both? Did one or both sides attempt to widen the conflict through horizontal or vertical escalation, or to increase its magnitude and duration by additional mobilization of resources? If an additional state or other parties intervened in the conflict, did this produce reassessment of policy and strategy? If there were adjustments in policy or strategy during the war, were these based on rational and timely reexamination of the relationship between the political objective and the means available?

6. War Termination

Did the war end because of the collapse of one of the belligerents, the capitulation of one of the sides, mutual exhaustion, or the negotiation of a settlement? If negotiations began before the end of hostilities, how well did military operations support diplomacy and vice versa? Did either side squander opportunities for a successful or partially successful end to the war? Did war termination occur only after a change of leadership on the losing side? If the war ended unexpectedly, did that surprise catch the victor unprepared to manage war termination? Did the winning side consider how far to go militarily to successfully terminate the conflict? Did it halt military operations prematurely or overstep the culminating point of victory? Were specific demands considered to fulfill their political objectives? How did allies manage competing interests? If there was a truce, did military or political leaders negotiate its terms? Did the terms of the truce shape the postwar settlement?

If the victorious belligerents had achieved the unlimited aim of overthrowing the enemy regime, were they ready to carry out occupation of the defeated country? If the victorious belligerents had pursued a limited aim and left the enemy regime in place, were they ready to execute, if necessary, a postwar policy of containment of the defeated country? Did the postwar settlement effectively incorporate the defeated state into the international system?

7. Winning the Peace and Preparing for War

To what extent did the postwar settlement satisfy the political objectives of the winning state or coalition? To what extent did the losing side accept its political and military losses? Did the end of the war leave the victor in a position to enforce peace? Had the victor planned adequately for the transition from war to peace?

Was the underlying conflict that gave rise to war resolved by that war? How did the outcome of interstate war affect the geostrategic position of the victors in relation to the vanquished? Did the victor attempt to reshape the international order? Did the members of the winning coalition maintain the collective will to enforce peace?

How were the lessons of the previous war absorbed into the policies, military thought, and doctrine of victorious, vanquished, and uncommitted powers? Did strategic leaders presume the next war would be similar to the last one? Did they strive to create conditions that would make the next war utterly dissimilar to the previous one? Was military-technological progress seen as likely to favor the offense or the defense in the next war? How did military and political leaders manage the transition from resolving a past war to preparing for a future conflict?

8. The International Dimension of Strategy

How successful were political and military leaders at seizing opportunities to isolate their adversaries from potential allies? What common interests or policies unified coalition partners? Did coalition partners have the same primary enemy and agree on strategy? What were the capabilities and limitations of each partner in the coalition? How effective was strategic coordination and burden sharing within a coalition? How freely did information, intelligence, and resources pass among its members? How important was coalition cohesion to the outcome of the war, and how robust was that cohesion? How did diplomacy contribute to coalition cohesion? Did coalition strategies solidify it or split it apart? Did these strategies strengthen or weaken the opposing coalition? Did allies act to support, restrain, or control one another? If a coalition disintegrated, was this the result of internal stress, external pressure, or both? Did coalition dynamics help or hinder efforts to match strategy to policy? What impact did coalition dynamics have on war termination? Did the winning coalition persist after the end of the war, and why?

Did war change the international system by changing the international distribution of power or by creating new institutions? What were the implications of the war for the belligerents’ political stability, social structure, economic viability, and military potential? Did the war stimulate activity by non-state actors?

9. The Economic and Material Dimensions of Strategy

What economic system did each country possess: predominantly agricultural, mercantile, industrial, or post-industrial? To what extent did government direct or control economic activity, and with what results? Did the defense-industrial base produce the weapons and military technology the country needed? Was a belligerent able to benefit from ongoing or recent waves of technological innovation? Did a gap open over time between strategic commitments and resources available to support those commitments? If so, what were the consequences of that gap for the country’s security?

How effectively did each belligerent mobilize its economic resources? How did a belligerent’s financial strength, natural resources, manufacturing plant, scientific expertise, and technological prowess affect its ability to wage war? Were belligerents able to manage financial constraints? What were the implications of a belligerent’s public finances for staying power in a long-term competition? Which of the belligerents had superior logistics for moving manpower and materiel to the theaters and sustaining forces? Was the outcome of the competition due more to material superiority or superior strategy?

If a belligerent adopted economic warfare, how appropriate was this strategy and how well was it integrated with other strategies? How adept were belligerents at overcoming attacks on their material capability to wage war?

10. Civil-Military Relations and the Institutional Dimension of Strategy

What were the roles, relationships, and functions of the institutions involved in developing strategy and managing national security? How did theater commanders fit into the overall chain of command? How were military forces organized? How well did that system facilitate planning, executing, and training for joint and combined warfare? How freely was information shared among military and civilian agencies?

How did rivalry among military services affect strategy and the presentation of a coherent military view on strategy to civilian leadership? Did organizational problems undermine civilmilitary relations? Did competition within the government or among its sections obscure military leaders’ understanding of the political objectives? How did lack of clarity or constancy in political aims affect civil-military relations? If political leaders demanded something from the military that it could not effectively deliver, or if they imposed stringent restraints on the use of force, how did military leadership respond? If military leaders proposed operations that promised to be militarily effective but entailed significant political risk, how did civilian leadership react?

How attuned were military leaders to the need to assess and manage political risk? How did the personalities of the key military and civilian leaders affect the civil-military relationship?

Did the transition from war to peace, or from one form of war to another, lead to institutional changes in a country’s national security system? How well did new national security institutions and processes perform in the next war? Were new institutions and old institutions able to work together effectively? Did institutional changes affect how the political and military leadership shared responsibility for strategy?

11. The Cultural and Social Dimensions of Strategy

How did a belligerent’s culture, society, ideology, and religion affect the strategy-policy match? Did a belligerent possess a discernable “strategic culture” or “way of war” and, if so, did this allow its adversary to predict and exploit its behavior? Did belligerents understand the values, social relationships, and institutions of all parties?

How did military power affect the course and outcome of any underlying ideological struggle? Did military or non-military factors have the greatest impact on the outcome? If the war involved a struggle for mass political allegiance, did culture, values, social structure, or religion give either belligerent an advantage? Did the existence of marginalized groups within the belligerent’s society create strategic vulnerabilities that its opponents could exploit? Did information operations or strategic communication reinforce or negate any such advantage? How did ethnic or religious passions affect the conduct and outcome of the competition or the war? How did ideas about race influence the political objectives, strategies, and operations of each belligerent? Did the existence of a racial hierarchy undermine the war effort? Was the war marked by terrorism or insurgency? Was it possible for external powers to resolve the conflict by military or diplomatic intervention?

Was the Clausewitzian triangle—the relationship among the government, the people, and the military—able to withstand battlefield reverses, catastrophic damage to the homeland, or the strain of protracted war? If the war was protracted, how successful was the victorious side in weakening its adversary’s society? Did military strategy deliver sufficient incremental dividends or periodic successes to maintain support for the war? Or did strategy diminish domestic support for the war? How effectively did belligerents mobilize and manage public opinion? Did communications media outside governmental control make it difficult for political leaders to manage public opinion at home and influence attitudes abroad? Did the “passions of the people” make it difficult for leaders to maintain the proper relationship between policy and strategy?

Course Introduction

In the waning days of the Vietnam War, Vice Admiral Stansfield Turner served as President of the Naval War College. He saw a glaring need to revolutionize the curriculum in professional military education. Rather than training officers, he sought to educate leaders. Admiral Turner argued: If you attempt to make this a prep school for your next duty assignment, you will have missed the purpose of being here. If we trained you for a particular assignment or type of duty, the value of this college would be short-lived. We want to educate you to be capable of doing well in a multitude of future duties…. Your objective here should be to improve your reasoning, logic, and analysis. The Strategy and Policy Course embodies Turner’s mission to place education over training by challenging students to grapple with the complex relationship among policy, strategy, and operations, lifting perspectives above the tactical level while sharpening critical thinking about joint matters. Critical strategic thinking constitutes the hallmark of the Strategy and Policy Course. We achieve this goal through graduate-level interdisciplinary seminars employing a unique methodology built upon two core components: the study of foundational theories of war, and close analysis of historical and contemporary case studies. The Strategy and Policy Course uses this case-study approach, integrating a diverse array of academic disciplines, including history, economics, political science, and security studies, to assess both historical and contemporary conflicts. This methodology exposes students to historical case studies in which senior political and military leaders, as well as staff planners, struggle to use the military and other instruments of national power to achieve the purpose and objectives of the state in both war and peacetime.

The course emphasizes the vital importance of orchestrating multinational cooperation while integrating all instruments of national power. Moreover, it instills in students the awareness and ability to perform comprehensive assessments at all stages of a conflict, and to communicate such assessments with clarity and precision. Finally, the course challenges students to think critically about the full spectrum of conflict: from prewar planning and the decision to go to war, to wartime adaptation, conflict termination, and the ultimate transition to peace. After examining past conflicts in a disciplined way, students emerge better equipped to grasp the values of the profession of arms espoused by the U.S. armed forces. Students comprehend more fully the capacity of U.S. military forces to conduct the full range of operations in pursuit of national interests. Moreover, students better understand why and how the U.S. military establishment is organized to plan, execute, and sustain joint, interagency, and multinational operations.

Theories of War, Strategy, and Great Power Competitions

Overview

One of the main goals of Professional Military Education is to develop “strategically minded joint warfighters who think critically.” Those seeking to hone their critical thinking skills can do so either through first-hand experience or study. For the military professional, firsthand experience can be a bloody process of real-world trial and error. The Strategy and Policy Course uses case studies to impart critical habits of thought in the classroom, where learning does not have such potentially catastrophic results. Though each case is unique, the sequence of cases is designed toward a cumulative outcome through the integration of theorists, course themes, strategic frameworks for analysis, and historical examples. The first case provides foundational concepts of the course, defines terminology so that students can communicate effectively, and, more importantly, it exposes students to several key course theorists.

Carl von Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, Sir Basil Liddell Hart and Alfred Thayer Mahan provide a theoretical and analytical foundation for the Strategy and Policy course. In future case studies, students will encounter additional theorists, including Julian S. Corbett, Thucydides, and Mao Zedong. To fully utilize these thinkers, it is important to grasp the value of theoretical writing.

These sometimes complementary, sometimes-conflicting works do not provide standardized answers. Instead, theorists impart common frames of reference and useful concepts for civilian and military leaders to utilize when integrating instruments of national power in the pursuit of political ends. They spark thought, stimulate debate, and promote creativity. Theory does not provide a one-size-fits-all answer; rather, theory contributes to the development of sound strategy. These theorists offer a point of departure from which we can think critically about the subsequent case studies and course themes.

First, the theorists provide methods for thinking through difficult problems. Book 2 of Clausewitz’s On War is particularly suggestive. In these pages, Clausewitz investigates the purpose of theory and introduces the concept of critical analysis to war. Rather than prescribing rigid rules, these theorists offer frameworks to guide judgment. Students should, however, understand that these methods of thinking can be applied to issues beyond the use of force and can assist with problem-solving in nearly every aspect of life. After all, Clausewitz’s critical analysis entails “the application of theoretical truths to actual events.” It requires “not just an evaluation of the means actually employed, but of all possible means.” Second, every theorist in the Strategy and Policy Course contends that war must serve a rational political purpose. This is not mere coincidence: the theorists span several millennia and represent different cultures, societies, and political systems. Strategy involves linking the ways to attain an end with the available means, in a competitive environment where adversaries react and respond. One cannot understand strategy without an appreciation of all three factors.

Third, the theorists present an expansive array of concepts. Their ideas and frameworks provide tools for analysis and ways to expand the student’s mental aperture. Though the theorists presented in the course wrote many years ago, their concepts remain relevant today. Sun Tzu’s injunction to know the enemy and know oneself parallels Clausewitz’s recommendations on prewar planning and lives on in our contemporary concept of “net assessment.” Moreover, Sun Tzu’s emphasis on advantageous positioning, superior speed, and surprise foreshadows many aspects of what is now called “maneuver warfare.” Likewise, Clausewitz’s maxim of concentrating forces against the enemy’s “center of gravity” still lies at the heart of U.S. joint military doctrine and planning processes. Liddell Hart’s concern with war termination and a better state of peace reflects personal experience of two 20th century world wars, but also a deep understanding of historical competitions between great powers.

The Naval War College’s own Alfred Thayer Mahan adds an additional layer, focusing on the role of navies, trade, and maritime commerce in building the capacities of states during both peace and war. His theories range from the level of grand strategy to that of naval tactics.

His grand strategic analysis explored the interrelationship of naval power, geography, social structure, economic organization, and governmental institutions. In the process, he developed the concept of sea power—a combination of naval might and financial and economic strength, which needed to be considered not only in times of war but also in times of peace. He argued that creating and sustaining sea power requires favorable geographic, economic, social, and political conditions. When addressing naval strategy, operations, and tactics, Mahan emphasized the aggressive employment of the fleet. Central to Mahan’s theory is a critical operational decision with enormous strategic importance: under what circumstances does it make strategic sense to risk one’s fleet? This necessitates exploring the ways sea power can influence a war’s outcome.

Specifically, Mahan believed sea power could have a decisive effect on the outcome of war.

Finally, each theorist describes an overarching way of war grounded in the context of the theorist’s time. Each wrote for a specific type of belligerent with definite instruments of power, and in a certain strategic environment. Clausewitz served Prussia, a continental power on the European mainland. The state’s primary instrument of national power was its army. His writings grapple with changes in warfare that occurred during the Napoleonic Wars. Sun Tzu’s writings reflect the instruments of power and conditions specific to the warring states of ancient China.

Mahan examines emerging trends in naval technology and commerce, relying on a careful study of sea power in the age of sail. Liddell Hart re-examines scholarship on strategy in the aftermath of two cataclysmic wars that ended several centuries of European dominance in global affairs.

Though the insights of the theorists have relevance beyond warfare of their specific era and their type of state, students of strategy should keep in mind the context in which each theorist wrote. It allows us to better conceptualize the strengths and limitations of their theories. Moreover, this helps us to understand some of the principal critiques levied against their writings.

Theoretical work does not provide definitive answers to strategic problems. Rather than answers, theory provides principles and concepts to consider when seeking solutions to complex problems. In that pursuit, we must not twist and distort the theorists into things they are not. Each theorist provides specific tools, and as students of strategy, we must seek the proper tool.

Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, and Liddell Hart offer complementary theories. Clausewitz provides a critical point of departure by clearly describing war as “nothing but the continuation of policy with other means.” As the title of his book suggests, he writes extensively on war. In some respects, his focus is the narrowest of all the theorists in the course, yet he provides a definition and description of war in its various parts. Unlike Clausewitz, who developed complex and reasoned arguments, Sun Tzu addresses strategy in concise, yet profound statements. His writings tend to stretch beyond the actual fighting. He emphasizes winning without fighting and his menu of options addresses the value of attacking an opponent’s strategy, and alliances, supporting his argument that victory is possible without bloodshed. Liddell Hart extrapolated from Clausewitz and Sun Tzu to posit a theory of “grand strategy”—an “all instruments of power” approach for nations and coalitions to assure positive political outcomes in peace and war.

Mahan discusses the role of the sea power in providing or supporting these other instruments – diplomatic influence in peace and war, economic prosperity as an underpinning of state power – as well as the role of navies in wartime. Geography plays an important role in shaping strategy and policy, and access to and exploitation of the sea provides benefits in both peace and war. This is important when addressing long-term competition between great powers.

These competitions require analysis of the interplay of strategic concepts and policy instruments, as well as careful strategic thinking in both war and peace.

In keeping with the cumulative nature of the course, this case provides critical frameworks for evaluating complex problems of strategy and policy that will be presented in the subsequent historical case studies. Rather than answers, this case study leaves us with questions and frameworks that allow students of strategy to fulfill the current military leadership’s expectation for “the development of strategically minded joint warfighters who think critically and can creatively apply military power to inform national strategy, conduct globally integrated operations, and fight under conditions of disruptive change.” 6

Essay & Discussion Questions (24)

  1. What does Clausewitz conceive to be the value of theory for strategic leaders in the profession of arms?
  2. What does Clausewitz mean by critical analysis? How can this concept aid those in the profession of arms as well as national security professionals when making strategy and policy decisions?
  3. Clausewitz emphasizes the need to understand the importance of three interrelated aspects of war: reason, passion, and the play of chance and creativity. How do the three concepts interact as part of a trinity? What value does the trinity have for a student of strategy who is attempting to understand individual wars, and does the trinity’s value change when approaching long-term competitions?
  4. Clausewitz, on page 69 of On War, recognizes two kinds of war, involving limited or unlimited objectives. How do they differ from each other?
  5. In Book 1, Chapter 1 of On War, Clausewitz makes a distinction between war in theory—which tends to escalate until all available forces are used—and war in reality. How do the two types of war differ from each other? Why are most wars waged with less than total effort?
  6. Some have suggested that technological advances may soon lift the “fog of war” completely, thus invalidating certain of Clausewitz’s most important insights. Do you agree?
  7. How does Clausewitz’s view of the proper relationship between war and politics compare to the views offered by Sun Tzu?
  8. What factors do Clausewitz and Sun Tzu tell the reader to assess when trying to better understand friends, enemies, neutrals, and even oneself?
  9. The authors of The Art of War and On War agree: though war can be studied systematically, strategic leadership is an art, not a science. What are the implications of this proposition for the study of strategy and policy?
  10. How do Clausewitz and Sun Tzu define war? In what ways do their respective definitions impact their theories?
  11. The Art of War says that “to subdue the enemy without fighting is the acme of skill,” while Clausewitz states that very limited and defensive objectives might be secured by the mere deployment of force. Are these two statements contradictory or complementary?
  12. Evaluate the role of intelligence in The Art of War and On War. Which view is more relevant today?
  13. What does Mahan mean by “sea power”? How can countries possessing sea power influence the international environment?
  14. What are Mahan’s six elements of sea power? How were these elements relevant at the time of his writing and how are they relevant today?
  15. What is “grand strategy?” How useful are Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, and Mahan for thinking about grand strategy? Does Liddell Hart’s definition reflect the thinking of either Clausewitz, Sun Tzu or Mahan?
  16. On page 131, Clausewitz states “we clearly see that the activities characteristic of war may be split into two main categories: those that are merely preparation for war, and war proper.” Does this mean that strategic principles cannot be applied to peacetime? Would Sun Tzu and/or Mahan agree?
  17. Sun Tzu argued that attacking an enemy’s strategy and disrupting an enemy’s alliances are the two preferred means of winning conflicts. How can these concepts be applied at the grand strategic level?
  18. How do Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, and Mahan believe that nations win wars?
  19. Liddell Hart considered “the object in war is to attain a better peace.” What did Hart consider to be a better state of peace? Would Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, and Mahan agree?
  20. Clausewitz emphasizes the primacy of politics in waging war. The Peloponnesian War: Peace, War, and Deterrence in a Long-Term Competition

Overview

Our first historical case study involves a war that may be unfamiliar to many students.

Thucydides, however, meant for his account of the decades-long conflict between a rising Athenian empire and the traditional hegemon, Sparta, to be “a possession for all time.” He succeeded. American political and military leaders from John Adams to George C. Marshall considered its lessons applicable to the security challenges of their own day. In this case study, our lens has been widened to include not only the Peloponnesian War (431-404 B.C.) that Thucydides wrote about, but also the long-term competition in which that war occurred.

Read the full case overview (15 more paragraphs)

The struggle of Athens and Sparta for hegemony lasted 75 years—from the initial defeat of the invading Persians by an Athenian-Spartan alliance in 479 B.C. to the final defeat of Athens by a Persian-Spartan alliance in 404 B.C. Vibrant Athens was on the rise for the first 50 years of this case study, prompting two wars with Sparta and its allies in the Peloponnesian League. These two former allies interacted along a continuum of competition from economic sanctions to outright conflict. Looming offshore was Persia, an ancient “superpower” that played the city-states of Greece against one another.

After the Athenian-Spartan alliance beat back the Persian invasion in 479 B.C., both citystates could have taken an equal share of the spoils. Sparta, however, withdrew and retrenched while Athens assumed leadership of the Delian League. Originally a confederation of maritime states created as an anti-Persian alliance, the Delian League slowly transformed into the Athenian empire, and Athenian wealth and power—especially maritime power—grew impressively. Sparta increasingly began to fear this growth and considered invasion to stop it.

A defection from Sparta’s alliance system in 460 B.C. triggered war between Athens and Sparta. Sparta and its allies tried to check growing Athenian power. Athens faced revolts within its empire, dispatched a doomed overseas expedition, was threatened by Spartan invasion, and was weakened by Persian intrigues. The war ended indecisively in 446 B.C. with a thirty-year treaty meant to prevent the same causes from triggering another war.

However, a stable peace proved elusive. Continued Athenian expansion unsettled Sparta and its allies. Several local crises gave Sparta the pretext to declare war in 431 B.C. despite the objections of its king, Archidamus. For the first seven years of the war, both alliances relied on their strengths. Athens used its maritime power to protect its lines of communications and launch small amphibious raids, while Sparta invaded the Athenian homeland annually. Neither side was able to terminate the war, however, and both found more success with modified strategies.

Athens applied sea power more aggressively and secured what became a major victory by capturing a group of Sparta’s elite citizen-soldiers. Unable to pursue its traditional strategy for fear of endangering its captured citizens, Sparta targeted Athenian allies and lines of communication, capturing a key source of Athens’ naval stores. An inconclusive battle and the deaths of both field commanders empowered the peace parties in each city state in 421 B.C. The resulting Peace of Nicias, named for the Athenian leader who brokered it, was supposed to last fifty years. But neither side completely fulfilled the treaty’s obligations, and

soon Athens was intriguing against Sparta while Sparta’s allies were encouraging it to resume the war. Athens failed to win a decisive land battle against Sparta in 418 B.C. and saw its dreams of an expanded empire crushed by the failure of the Sicilian Campaign in 413 B.C. After this defeat, Persia finally chose sides. Persian ships and money allowed Sparta to rapidly integrate sea power on an unprecedented scale. By the end of the war, Athens was again dealing with repeated allied revolts, the loss of a large expeditionary force in Sicily, a permanent Spartan garrison near Athens, and the active involvement of Persia on the side of Sparta. The destruction of Athens’ remaining naval forces in 405 B.C. led its leaders to sue for peace the following year.

The Peloponnesian War is especially rich for illustrating many of the concepts presented in the Strategy and Policy course. This case allows students to consider the interrelationship between war termination, winning the peace and preparing for war, and the decision for war. Of the three major conflicts in this case study, only the Persian Wars ended without a formal, negotiated settlement. During the Peloponnesian War from 431-404 B.C., both Athens and Sparta rejected multiple peace offers from the other side, and neither fully complied with the terms of the Peace of Nicias. It is worth examining whether these failed because one side demanded too much (or perhaps too little) politically or did not go far enough militarily.

The Greek city-states were never able to transform their military victories into permanent peace agreements. In some cases, the underlying reasons for the conflict remained unresolved, preventing a more lasting peace from taking hold. The treaty agreed to at the end of the first Athenian-Spartan conflict in 446 B.C. was specifically structured to prevent the tensions that led to war, but in 431 B.C. the Peloponnesian War began under remarkably similar circumstances.

These issues prompted Athens and Sparta to strengthen and realign their coalitions and continually prepare for the next war. As a result, each power deliberately chose to initiate, or reinitiate, hostilities. Thucydides presents several of these decisions—the beginning of the Second Peloponnesian War and the Athenian invasion of Sicily—as debates within one or more of the alliances. It is worth asking whether these decisions were based on new policy aims, unfulfilled policy aims from the previous war, or poor war termination and peace enforcement.

Similarly, students should evaluate not only how well the victors “won” the peace, but how all sides prepared for the next war.

Thucydides is also one of the key theorists in this course. Although he did not originate the theory of coercion, and neither Athens nor Sparta thought of their strategies in terms of deterrence or compellence, analysts have found examples of these modern concepts in his history. After all, Athens and Sparta spent much of the war maneuvering around various treaties, and they often attempted to intimidate or cajole their allies and enemies through their speeches and actions. Both Athens and Sparta used military, diplomatic, economic, and information tools to support their efforts in war and peace. Students should consider whether coercion failures contributed to both Athenian and Spartan decisions for war or their inability to win the peace.

Thucydides is sometimes called the founder of realism or power politics, and students can trace the evolution of this concept as well as its implementation through Thucydides’ speeches, many of which present both realism and its alternatives. Additionally, the motivations of honor, fear, and self-interest may serve as a sort of “Thucydidean trinity,” much like

Clausewitz’s trinity of passion, chance, and reason. Embedded within the text are key concepts that may offer frameworks to view later cases, such as net assessment, the policy-strategy match, and the difference between proximate and ultimate causes.

In addition, focusing on Thucydides’ descriptions of the challenges faced by democratic Athens during the Peloponnesian War, provides possible lessons for the United States today. To what extent do modern democracies embody the characteristics of ancient Athens, and how much can we learn from the Athenian experience? If Clausewitz and Sun Tzu were right to suggest that self-knowledge is the foundation of any effective policy and strategy, then is Thucydides’ account of the rise and fall of Athens a worthwhile starting point for understanding the problems modern democracies experience in long-term competitions?

Finally, this case introduces international relations (IR) theory. Building on the study of strategic theorists like Clausewitz, Sun Tzu, Mao, Corbett, and Mahan, IR theory expands the strategist’s toolkit by introducing additional concepts and theoretical lenses to explain the behavior of states and international actors. This framework helps students organize complex information, isolate the factors with the greatest explanatory power, and better understand the dynamics of competition, conflict, and cooperation. Students will explore key concepts alongside the major theoretical perspectives of realism, liberalism, and constructivism. Ultimately, students will apply these theories to their analysis of the Peloponnesian War, as well as all subsequent historical and contemporary cases in the course.

The Wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon: Maritime Versus Continental Security Strategies

Overview

Between 1793 and 1815, Britain and France struggled for hegemony over Europe and dueled for supremacy over the world’s oceans. Britain, as a maritime power in possession of a dominant navy, sought unrivalled command of the seas. France, with its own maritime interests, clashed repeatedly with Britain. With a powerful army, France under Napoleon sought to impose its imperium on Europe. Although Britain possessed a smaller army, British leaders consistently opposed French plans on the continent. The challenges faced by continental and maritime powers in overcoming their asymmetric deficiencies and applying their strengths contributed to the protracted nature of the wars considered in this case study. Unlike the Peloponnesian War, in which the maritime power Athens lost command of the sea and suffered defeat, in the Wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon the maritime power Britain maintained its command of the sea and in combination with the continental powers Austria, Prussia, and Russia eventually defeated Napoleonic France.

The wars examined in this case study—The Wars of the French Revolution (1792-1802) and the Napoleonic Wars (1803-1815)—were the final pair of conflicts in a longstanding great power competition between Britain and France. These wars were in part precipitated by the destabilizing influence of the French Revolution. Britain remained throughout the stalwart opponent of Revolutionary France and French imperial designs under Napoleon. In the opening stages of the conflict, William Pitt the Younger guided British policy and strategy. His successors would follow his basic strategy of maximizing Britain’s naval power, along with Britain’s strengths in finance, industry, and commerce, while minimizing its weakness on land by supporting anti-French coalitions.

Read the full case overview (7 more paragraphs)

The Wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon offer a valuable opportunity for enhancing student understanding of a broad range of important S&P course concepts and themes to include: the instruments of national power, the interrelationship of policy, strategy, and operations, and the international dimension of strategy. The case study also emphasizes other broad concepts. One concept is the fundamental difference between what is necessary to compel an adversary to sue for peace and what is required to make peace durable. The protracted nature of the wars in this case allows students to contrast operational with strategic success and underscores the interplay of civil and military leadership in successful war termination. Napoleon is often ranked among the greatest military commanders in the history of warfare, despite losing his empire and dying in exile. As Napoleon rose to prominence in the 1790s, he increasingly blurred the lines between military and political leadership by becoming First Consul through a coup d’état in 1799. In 1804, he took the additional step of becoming Emperor of the French. As emperor, he won stunning battlefield victories, including Austerlitz, Jena, and Friedland. Napoleon was not only a successful commander but also the head of state, wielding the political power to terminate individual wars. A stable peace, however, eluded him. Why did Napoleon, one of the greatest battlefield commanders in history, suffer defeat and forced abdication? And how did Britain, along with Napoleon’s other opponents, achieve lasting peace? Another topic in policy and strategy highlighted in this case is the influence of political culture on strategy. The French Revolution transformed politics and consequently the conduct of

wars. The French Revolution altered the relationship between the government and the people and transformed the organization and development of the military. The military was no longer composed of long-service volunteers and foreign mercenaries, but of citizen soldiers. Command was no longer the exclusive preserve of those of noble family lineages but open to all who demonstrated talent. Ideas of liberty, equality, and nationalism created powerful motivations that turned the population from subjects of a king into citizens of a nation. Revolutionaries harnessed these motivations through the levée en masse, organizing France for warfare on a scale previously unknown. This created a nation in arms with the entire state focused on waging a new way of war that encouraged boldness.

This case study introduces the sea power theories of Sir Julian Corbett and re-emphasizes the writings of Alfred Thayer Mahan. These major theorists on strategy examine the challenge of winning naval mastery and understanding the strategic effects derived from commanding the maritime commons. Sir Julian Corbett drew heavily upon Clausewitz’s On War to develop a distinctive analysis of how maritime powers fight and win wars. At sea, Corbett believed the key objective from which all other effects flowed was the need to obtain “command of the sea.” Corbett used Britain’s experience in the Napoleonic Wars to show the strategic impact that a maritime power can have in influencing the outcomes of wars on land. Although the British army was not as strong as those of the other European great powers, the mobility provided by the Royal Navy allowed the army to exert influence on the war’s outcome. Britain’s joint capabilities allowed for opening and closing secondary theaters. After several false starts, Britain conducted a textbook example of joint and combined strategy in the Iberian Peninsula under the Duke of Wellington’s leadership. The British campaigns in Spain and Portugal provided Corbett with an ideal case study to illustrate his strategic theories.

Alfred Thayer Mahan is famous for his examination of sea power. Mahan served as a professor and second president of the Naval War College. The excerpt from The Influence of Sea Power on the French Revolution and Empire, 1793-1812 illustrates key aspects of Mahan’s strategic theories. Mahan developed the concept of sea power, which is a grand strategy that emphasizes the interrelationship of naval power, geopolitics, society, the economy, and governmental institutions. When addressing naval strategy, operations, and tactics, Mahan emphasized the aggressive employment of the fleet. He argued that Admiral Horatio Nelson, Britain’s famous naval leader, was the true embodiment of sea power largely because of his unerring quest for battle and the effects Britain obtained from his victories. This case study allows students to analyze under what circumstances it makes strategic sense for continental and maritime powers to risk their respective fleets. This necessitates exploring the ways naval power can influence a war’s outcome. Can this influence be decisive? For example, the Battle of Trafalgar, fought on October 21, 1805, has mythic status, but what strategic advantages did Britain derive from Trafalgar that it did not already possess?

The prolonged period of warfare covered in this case allows for an examination of the strategic effects of economic and financial instruments of national power, coalition warfare, and the challenge of making a durable peace. Napoleon’s Continental System sought to monopolize continental trade for the benefit of France while severing Britain’s economic ties with the European continent. Britain employed its own instruments of economic warfare in retaliation against the Continental System. These attempts by Britain and France to damage their

opponent’s economy resulted in an escalation of the war, as their objectives expanded, and economic warfare drew additional states into the conflict. Although Britain played a prominent role in the coalitions against France, the other European great powers provided most of the land forces. Only in 1813 did a final coalition form that proved capable of defeating Napoleon. A comparison of the success of the final coalition to the previous failures reveals both the prerequisites for coalition cohesion as well as high barriers to coalition unity. The statesmen who created the final coalition against Napoleonic France endeavored to transition from a wartime coalition to one capable of enforcing peace and providing long-term stability. Before the gates of Paris in 1814 and then at the Congress of Vienna in 1815, European political leaders planned a comprehensive postwar settlement to ensure stability through the satisfaction of essential national interests. The European great powers created a system of international order that produced a period of relative peace.

The Wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon offer important lessons for political and military leaders today. Study of these wars will fulfill the PME goal of Program Learning Outcome 4: current doctrine rests upon theoretical foundations drawn from careful study of the history of these wars, including the theories of Clausewitz, Corbett, and Mahan. The sharp ideological divide created by the French Revolution, a division between proponents of the values of liberal democracy and those supporting traditional authoritarianism, continues to exist as illustrated by Vladimir Putin’s recent war on Ukraine and the West more generally. Great power competition has revived and remains as dangerous to peace today as it was in the era of the Wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon. The theories of Clausewitz, Mahan, and Corbett, derived from careful study of the lessons of the wars covered in this case, continue to inform current strategic thinking. Finally, an historical appreciation of the Wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon can be of value to leaders today when navigating the treacherous waters of the current geopolitical environment.

Imperial Germany from the Wars of Unification to the First World War — The Rise and Fall of a Peer Competitor

Overview

This case study examines several strategy and policy course themes and concepts, starting with Winning the Peace and Preparing for War. The readings and lectures provide the basis for an in-depth inquiry into the strategic effects of shifting power balances within the international environment, assessing the extent to which it is possible to shape the foreign policy choices and strategies of rising great powers, and the failure to deter a major global war. This case on imperial Germany considers the critically important question of why the leaders of a thriving, major industrial and trading power, which stood to gain economically and politically by adopting the role of a peaceful international stakeholder, chose instead to provoke wars in an attempt to dominate regional rivals and pursue global aspirations.

War marked the emergence of Imperial Germany as a great power during the nineteenth century. The north German state of Prussia, with its capital in Berlin, fought three limited wars known as the Wars of German Unification—the Danish War of 1864, the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, and the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-1871—to forge a united Germany under its rule. The architect of these wars was the legendary statesman, Otto von Bismarck. Bismarck’s strategic leadership receives close attention in this case study. While serving as Prussia’s Minister-President, Bismarck proved a master at managing the delicate policy-strategy relationship in limited wars. His goal was to make Prussia the dominant power in Germany. Bismarck understood that, to defeat Prussia’s rivals in war, he needed to calibrate objectives, effectively integrate military operations and diplomacy, and balance the triangular relationship between the people, the government, and the army. Bismarck faced and took great risks in this. There was always the danger of defeat on the battlefield, protracted war, or escalation to a wider, general European conflict. Bismarck sought to control escalation in ever more ambitious wars and prevent great-power intervention against Prussia. Studying Bismarck, this master of wars fought for limited aims, provides insights into the making of policy and strategy by a country that seeks to challenge the international status quo without provoking escalation into a wider war.

Read the full case overview (5 more paragraphs)

The story of German policy and strategy after Bismarck left office in 1890 provides a cautionary tale of how a great power can bring ruin upon itself when a new generation of leaders adopts self-defeating strategies that provoke a strong, determined coalition of encircling enemies. For a period of some twenty years after the Franco-Prussian War, while Bismarck still held the reins of power, Germany functioned as a satiated power on the international stage, trying to preserve the peace and consolidate the gains won in its earlier wars. After 1890, Germany’s economy made impressive strides, becoming an economic powerhouse, with remarkable growth exhibited by its industry and foreign trade. Germany, already possessing the best army in Europe, sought to acquire a powerful navy as well by harnessing the wealth provided by its growing economy. Its rulers wanted to translate this increasing strength in “hard power” into enhanced international standing and security. This new course was called Weltpolitik. In this attempt, they badly miscalculated, putting at risk Germany’s considerable economic and technological achievements, and bringing together a powerful coalition of adversaries intent on stopping Berlin’s bid to establish hegemony over Europe.

Bismarck’s successors pushed for greater policy aims that threatened to overturn the existing international order. The German government pursued an international confrontation to bring on war during the summer of 1914 and overthrow the European balance of power. Its prewar military preparations and strategic planning proved inadequate to achieve these ambitious policy goals. Germany’s aims in the First World War came to reflect all too accurately the ambitions of nationalist extremists who sought to impose German hegemony. This dramatic escalation of political aims galvanized Germany’s enemies to fight all the harder, resulting in a war fought for high stakes and with very high casualties. At the end of that hideous struggle, Imperial Germany, less than fifty years after its founding, suffered defeat and revolution. This case study, by examining the Instruments of National Power, the International Dimension of Strategy, and the Decision for War, seeks to illuminate what led to the triumphal emergence of Imperial Germany in the nineteenth century and to its later devastating defeat in the twentieth. Another key aspect of this case is to examine the interaction among technological innovation, geostrategic position, geopolitical environment, naval strategy, and operational doctrines for waging warfare at sea. The German navy undertook a long-term transformation of its operational capabilities, changing from a coastal defense force to an interdiction force that could strike at a distance against critical shipping lanes. This transformation posed a serious security challenge to Great Britain, the world’s leading naval power, undermining its ability to retain command of the maritime commons. This case delves into the attitudes of political and naval leaders toward naval force structure, maritime strategy, and risk in the employment of naval forces and how they sought to manage that risk.

At the end of the nineteenth century, Germany’s leaders concluded that to attain their foreign policy and security ambitions required building a powerful battle fleet to pose a direct threat to the very center of British power. Germany wanted to coerce Britain’s leaders away from joining any hostile coalition of great powers. Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, the German secretary of the navy, devised the strategic blueprint for this strategy. Meanwhile, Britain sought to deter Germany’s rulers from embarking on an aggressive war to establish a German hegemony over Europe. Both countries failed in their aims. Germany’s attempt to overturn Britain’s command of the maritime commons also led to confrontation with the United States during the First World War. President Woodrow Wilson worked to dissuade Germany from embarking on a campaign of unrestricted submarine warfare. This case provides an opportunity to examine how major powers interact with each other in the international system and why it is so difficult to devise a strategy that can successfully shape the actions of emerging competitors at strategic crossroads. The strategic effects of transformations in warfare form another important part of this case study. German military leaders designed and built armed forces to fight short-duration, high-intensity conflicts. The military professionalism of Helmuth von Moltke, chief of the Prussian and later German general staff, made possible the victories achieved in the Wars of German Unification. Railways, the telegraph, rapid-fire rifles, and longer-range artillery transformed the conduct of operations and increased battlefield lethality. The Prussian army capitalized on these developments to gain a military edge on its adversaries and achieve rapid victories. At sea, Germany adopted a disruptive strategy that employed submarines as weapons of commerce destruction, striking at British and neutral merchant shipping. Instead of defeating Britain, however, this radical strategy failed. To combat the German submarine menace, Britain

adopted convoys for the protection of merchant shipping, blunting the damage inflicted by German submarines. Germany’s actions at sea provoked the intervention of the United States in the fighting, blunting Germany’s efforts to quickly win the war at sea. Military transformation is no substitute for strategic wisdom.

Perhaps no case study in civil-military relations provides a more sobering example of the adverse strategic consequences that can result from a breakdown in the proper relationship between statesman and soldier. World War I shows how strategic choices, for good or ill, result from the actions of decision makers who bring differing bureaucratic backgrounds, strategic conceptions, and personalities to their deliberations. Bismarck used war to outmaneuver his domestic political foes, who wanted to control government policies by asserting the power of the Prussian parliament and the primacy of the rule of law. Later, during the First World War, deep disputes wracked the German political and military leadership. These disagreements pitted the chancellor against the army’s chief of staff and the navy’s leadership, as well as front-line theater military commanders against the High Command. Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg and General Erich Ludendorff emerged as national heroes, owing to their battlefield victories over Russia on the Eastern Front. They used their popularity to establish what amounted to a military dictatorship by the middle of the war. Militarism, as represented by Hindenburg and Ludendorff, provided the driving force behind Germany’s actions and self-defeating strategic behavior during the First World War. Examining Germany underscores how war can distort the relationship among a country’s people, government, and armed forces with disastrous consequences. Finally, this case gives students excellent opportunities to consider the strategic effects of operations to enhance warfighting effectiveness. Key operations such as the Schlieffen Plan, the Dardanelles Campaign, Germany’s unrestricted U-boat campaign, the Verdun and Somme offensives, and Germany’s final Spring Offensive are covered in detail to this end. This approach allows the case to uniquely bring together the elements of analysis in PLO 4, integrating theory, history, concepts of sea power, and doctrine.

The Interwar World: The Challenges of Winning the Peace, and the Re-Emergence of Great Power Competition

Overview

The 1920s and 1930s present instructive parallels to our contemporary security environment. Unlike other cases in the Strategy and Policy Course, this one does not focus on a major war or series of wars. Instead, it addresses a period between major great power conflicts. It asks questions of how states sought to “win the peace” in the aftermath of the First World War, and why those same states began preparing for war in the 1930s. Although the case broadly addresses the interwar world, Britain receives particular emphasis to organize and focus the case. American leaders today can learn much from how Britain tried to defend commitments around the world against rising revisionist powers while laboring under significant political and economic constraints.

This case study emphasizes several important concepts. These include the challenge of creating a lasting peace; the lingering impact of war on societies and economies; the difficulty in balancing security challenges with the available resources and instruments of national power; the influence of ideology on strategic decision-making; and the reemergence of great-power hostility.

Read the full case overview (5 more paragraphs)

We begin with efforts to secure a lasting peace after the First World War. The war had exhausted European states and empires. Four empires—Germany, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire—had collapsed, and the British and French empires were grievously wounded. A complex series of treaties collectively known as the Versailles peace settlement attempted to satisfy the victors and prevent another European war. Unfortunately, it soon proved impossible for the victors to enforce the peace. Even among the victors, there was a lack of will to commit the human and material resources required to rebuild the postwar world order. Britain and France emerged from the war weakened, while the United States refused to undertake any political or military responsibility for upholding the post war international system. The Versailles settlement appeared to be functioning well in the relatively prosperous 1920s. The critical blow to the new collective security system was the Great Depression. It began with a financial crisis in the United States that reverberated across the globe during the 1930s, causing profound economic and political turmoil. International trade plummeted and unemployment spiked, and the resulting economic weakness shaped how governments confronted rising security challenges. The failure of democratic states to deal effectively with the economic challenges of the postwar order discredited both liberalism and capitalism. Extremist parties found a fertile political landscape in the 1930s. The First World War destroyed the moral foundations of the pre-war social, economic, and political order of the Western powers. New ideologies, including communism and fascism, seemed to promise an exit from the failures of liberal, democratic, and constitutional politics. Fascist leaders, including the Nazis in Germany, implemented new social programs and economic plans that seemed to mitigate the worst ravages of the Great Depression. Communism, meanwhile, appeared to offer an attractive alternative to a broken capitalist system.

Leaders of these rival systems also adopted aggressive foreign policy objectives, including upending the political and territorial arrangements of Versailles. These revisionist powers included those that suffered defeat in the First World War, such as Germany and the Soviet Union. Ostensibly victorious powers also opted for new authoritarian regimes, including Italy and Japan, who believed their allies had cheated them of their rightful spoils and place in the world.

Confronting them, the democratic powers were divided and lacked either the political will, or the military means to enforce the peace settlement. The United States was wary of international commitments and ambivalent to the global order that Britain and France were trying to maintain. Britain was constrained by a weak post-war economy, geopolitical overextension, a population fatigued by war, and an elite committed to maintaining the empire. France, suffering from severe physical damage from the First World War, was unable to enforce German compliance to Versailles or to construct alliances that could successfully deter Berlin and stabilize the European system.

The example of Britain juggling multiple international challenges while adapting to domestic political and economic upheaval is instructive for the United States today. The empire expanded in the aftermath of the First World War, creating new policing and defense burdens at the very moment that popular support for an interventionist foreign policy waned and London faced pressure to reduce its defense expenditures. The British experience in the interwar world also provides insights into the difficulties military organizations face when confronted by multiple and sometimes contradictory challenges in peacetime. Britain had transformed its conventional military capabilities during the Great War, but this capability eroded rapidly as the army returned to constabulary roles in the Empire. The capabilities required for imperial policing were necessary for maintaining the British Empire but were unsuitable for great power conflict, and Britain lacked the capacity to preserve both sets of skills.

Meanwhile, there was parallel unravelling of regional stability in East Asia. Each great power in the region was revisionist: China was struggling to reunify in the aftermath of a nationalist revolution and maintain its territorial integrity against colonial encroachment, Russia was newly ruled by a Bolshevik pariah regime determined to secure itself against a two-front war, and Imperial Japan sought to acquire territory and resources it believed would guarantee the future of its growing population and rise as a great power. Without a meaningful constituency for status quo, the seeds of a highly destructive Pacific War were sown. Events in Asia ensured that the coming war would be truly global in a way that even the Great War was not. This case study encourages students to confront the hard choices that must be made by strategists even in peacetime. Choosing between unpalatable options, considering tradeoffs between different choices, and facing growing threats with a static or declining resource base are profound and difficult problems for decision makers. Anticipating the impact of changes in the global economic system, of emerging authoritarian ideologies, and of military commitments in secondary theaters remains a concern for contemporary military and political leaders in a period of renewed great power competition.

The Second World War and the Rise of the Superpowers

Overview

The Second World War is rich with lessons in policy and strategy relevant to understanding the contemporary international security environment. It was a global great-power conflagration with fighting in major theaters of operations on the world’s oceans, in Europe, on the mainland of Asia, and across the Middle East and North Africa. This case study builds on the previous examination of the interwar era by analyzing the victories achieved by Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan in the war’s opening stages. Despite early successes, the tide turned against the Axis as a powerful coalition formed against them, determined not only to roll back their conquests but to overthrow the regimes that started the war. This case gives special emphasis to the role of the United States and the Soviet Union in bringing about Axis defeat. Triumphant in war, the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as rival superpowers on the world stage, a rivalry that shaped the international system for the next forty years.

The Second World War opened with Germany and Japan gaining striking operational successes that conquered vast empires. In Asia, Japan was dissatisfied with the existing international order. Japan aspired to be the dominant great power in Asia, with an empire that provided it with markets and key resources. To achieve these aims, Japan expanded its empire by invading Manchuria in 1931 and then attacked Nationalist China in 1937. The Japanese army won victory after battlefield victory over the Chinese forces arrayed against it, capturing the Nationalist capital of Nanjing and seizing large swarths of China’s territory. These victories, however, were not enough to break the will of Chiang Kai-shek, the leader of Nationalist China, or of the Chinese people, who fought on.

The United States responded to Japan’s aggression in China with increasingly harsh economic sanctions. When Japan invaded French Indochina to cut the most important remaining supply route to China, the United States responded with a total oil embargo. Japan responded with an offensive to drive the Western powers out of Asia via simultaneous attacks across the region in December 1941. The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor galvanized the American people to fight as the United States entered the war. The ensuing conflict provides an example of warfare in the maritime domain between major industrial powers in a theater of operations similar to today's Indo-Pacific Command. Joint and coalition operations raged from eastern India and the Bay of Bengal across the Pacific to Alaska and south to Australia. With the bulk of the Japanese army tied down on the Asian mainland fighting in China and Burma, or deterring the Soviets, the Japanese navy bore the brunt of war against the United States. In Europe, Hitler’s Germany embarked on wars of conquest that threatened the international balance of power. Under the 1939 Nazi-Soviet Pact, Germany and the Soviet Union divided Eastern Europe into spheres of influence. This collaboration enabled Hitler’s legions to win lightning victories over Poland, Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, and (most stunning of all) France. Great Britain held out against the Nazi onslaught, despite heavy bombing of the British homeland by the Luftwaffe. Led by Winston Churchill, Britain continued the struggle in the hope that the United States would enter the war to overturn Germany’s conquests and overthrow the Nazi regime. Britain’s strategic fortunes turned for the better in June 1941 when the Nazi-Soviet partnership collapsed, and Hitler suddenly turned against Stalin to stake out an empire in the east in pursuit of Lebensraum (“living space”). Operation Barbarossa

inflicted grievous losses on the Soviet Red Army, conquered enormous amounts of territory, and brought German troops within sight of Moscow by December 1941.

But Moscow did not fall. The Soviet state had undertaken extensive preparations for a coming war during the interwar period. The Red Army’s order of battle was much stronger, and it possessed more world-class weaponry than Germany had anticipated. Despite enormous losses, the Soviets continued to field fighting forces, whereas German forces were ground down by the time they reached Moscow’s suburbs. Stalin also redeployed units from the Soviet Far East to defend the capital. These fresh troops spearheaded an offensive in bitterly cold weather that drove the Germans back. With German reversals before Moscow and Leningrad, Germany and the Soviet Union became locked in a grisly war that tested the political, social, and economic stamina of both countries, as well as the operational prowess of their armed forces. The conflict on the Eastern Front ended with the Red Army inflicting crushing losses on Germany’s army and marching on Berlin. Stalin would boast that the Soviet system had passed the test of war and emerged victorious. The cost of triumph was, however, hideously high in blood and treasure: including the loss of nearly thirty million Soviet lives. This massive struggle provides critical insights into contemporary European security.

Japan’s Asian advance and the German invasion of the Soviet Union brought together a Grand Alliance consisting of China, Great Britain, the Soviet Union, and the United States. While Japan and Germany gained early victories, the tide turned in 1942 when American, British, and Soviet armed forces began to fight more effectively and go on the offensive. This change in fortunes occurred even before the United States had fully mobilized its war economy. Successful campaigns in North Africa and the Solomon Islands set the stage for massive amphibious operations in both the Pacific and Europe. In addition, Britain and the United States conducted air offensives against the German and Japanese homelands that had important strategic effects in diverting weapons and personnel from fighting fronts, hurting their economies, and disrupting their societies. Politically, the issue of where and when the United States and Britain should open the Second European Front tested the cohesion of the Grand Alliance until D-Day in June 1944. By coordinating their offensive operations and economic resources, the Grand Alliance achieved the complete defeat of Germany and Japan. This case study highlights the international dimension of strategy theme and the challenges of strategic integration within a coalition.

This case study also highlights the economic and material dimensions of strategy. The Second World War showcased technological and military innovation by the combatants, the emergence of new military domains, the enormous logistic burdens involved in fighting a global contest, the industrial demands of modern war, the mobilization challenges of protracted war, the impact of war on societies and civilian populations, and the creation of atomic weapons used in war termination. The Axis powers sought to make conquest pay and increase their own combat power by extracting resources from conquered territories and peoples. On the Allied side, Britain and the United States devoted considerable domestic resources to long-range bomber forces to undertake devastating air attacks on Germany and Japan. Sharing resources and technology knit together the Grand Alliance and was critical to victory over the Axis. American Lend Lease increased the Soviet Union’s warfighting strength and offensive punch. Food supplied by Lend

Lease helped feed Soviet citizens and soldiers while American motor vehicles increased the mobility and fighting effectiveness of the Red Army.

State-funded technological change generated new means of waging war. After the first important use of tanks, aircraft, and submarines in the First World War, mechanized warfare, strategic bombing, carrier strikes, and unrestricted submarine warfare became central to military action in the Second World War. Germany and Japan used innovative technologies to achieve remarkable operational success from 1940 to 1942, even though their early advantage did not last. Germany’s later development of ballistic missiles and jet aircraft heralded a change in the character of warfare. Of special importance for the future, the United States, in cooperation with Britain, developed the first atomic weapons. American and British leaders feared that Germany might develop nuclear weapons and gain a decisive first-strike edge for winning the war. , Britain and the United States won this first nuclear arms race, and from this point onward nuclear weapons affect every case onward in the Strategy and Policy Course. Nuclear weapons and ballistic missiles remain a most potent danger in today’s international security environment. Global force management is another crucial element of this case study, which examines the strategic concepts and courses of action that leaders considered during the war. Strategies such as “Europe-first” (proposed in 1940-1941 by Admiral Harold Stark, U.S. Chief of Naval Operations) raise the question of how to sustain alliance efforts over the long-term to achieve national security ends. Several strategic challenges confronted the United States: German and Japanese actions in 1940-1941; Stalin’s maneuvering during the war to expand Soviet control over territory; as well as the pull of offensive operations to maintain strategic initiative in the war against Japan. Successful leaders must manage the risks and rewards involved in the allocation of forces and resources among different theaters.

Finally, this case study examines the rise of the United States as the world’s leading sea power. At the war’s outset, Britain had the world’s strongest navy while Japan fielded a powerful naval force, that pioneered in carrier aviation and developed powerful new weapons and platforms. Defeating the Axis required winning the contest for the world’s oceans. The United States Navy fought alongside British and Canadian fleets to defeat the German threat to Atlantic sea lanes which had done immense damage to Allied shipping. The victory in the North Atlantic was a precondition for the bringing about the offensive drives required to crush Nazi Germany. In the Pacific, the United States built up the carrier and amphibious forces that surged across the region and broke through the island chains that Japan hoped would shield its empire. The Navy fought and won the battles of Coral Sea, Midway, Guadalcanal, Philippine Sea, Leyte Gulf, and Okinawa against a determined and skillful Japanese opponent. The Pacific War was the most intense maritime conflict ever fought. Near its end, Japanese leaders resorted to kamikaze tactics, which foreshadowed naval warfare in the age of precision strike. The struggle to command the world’s sea lanes is worthy of any history told by Alfred Thayer Mahan or Sir Julian Corbett. This victory rested on the mobilization of the industrial plant necessary to build and repair fighting ships and merchant shipping, development and adoption of technologies, and innovation in doctrine for the employment of naval forces. Studying the United States Navy and the naval campaigns of the Second World War provide valuable insights for understanding current efforts to increase shipbuilding capacity, to build up naval strength, to embrace new technologies, and to develop new operational concepts for warfighting.

Case VIIEarly Cold WarLectures Jan 19, 21 · Seminars Jan 19, 26, 27Case study
The Early Cold War: National Security, Ideology, and Domestic Politics, 1945–1953

Overview

The following three cases all deal with the Cold War. Because this was a complex, global affair, it is impossible to cover every historical detail. Accordingly, the next three cases will emphasize U.S., Soviet, and Chinese conceptions of strategy and how they related to the global balance of power at various points during the Cold War, and how they evolved in response to changes in both international and domestic politics.

The Grand Alliance of World War II unraveled because the United States and the Soviet Union had divergent ideas about war termination, the division of the defeated nations and their empires, and the postwar international order. These differences, combined with the end of the US atomic monopoly and communist victory in the Chinese Civil War, contributed to the outbreak of war in Korea. Among other important things, this case gives students the opportunity to explore the Chinese Communist Party’s victory in the civil war under the leadership of Mao Zedong and whether the United States could have done more to prevent it.

Read the full case overview (14 more paragraphs)

This case study also offers an excellent opportunity to examine the role of leadership, as Franklin Roosevelt’s death brought his inexperienced and out-of-the-loop vice-president Harry S. Truman to power. Truman’s subsequent dealings with the Soviets were more confrontational than cooperative. Joseph Stalin saw Eastern Europe as a buffer and Germany as a source of reparations for a nation decimated by the Nazi invasion. Truman and his allies, especially Churchill, saw Soviet domination of Eastern Europe as a threat to the West, or in Churchill’s famous speech, as an Iron Curtain dividing the war-devastated continent.

As World War II ended, the United States attempted to engineer a world where great power competition that had precipitated both world wars would no longer exist. The U.S. created an international order to promote economic growth and mechanisms to arbitrate conflict rather than engage in the costly business of war. Truman sought to rein in defense spending, but even his cuts yielded the largest force in American history. The Soviet Union rejected the U.S.dominated world order and resumed the ideological struggle of the prewar years while pursuing traditional Russian foreign policy aims in Turkey, Iran, China, and Korea. Rather than create a lasting great-power peace, such as the Congress of Vienna after Napoleon’s defeat, the United States and the Soviet Union waged a new great power competition, the Cold War. During the Cold War both sides engaged each other indirectly through limited wars while managing a new set of technologies, nuclear weapons, whose use threatened global rather than simply national destruction.

The United States began postwar planning even before it entered World War II. U.S. officials focused on the development of institutions and arrangements that would rebuild the global economy and enable the reconstruction of nations devastated by the war in Europe and Asia. At the Bretton Woods conference, the U.S. and its allies, including the Soviet Union, created a postwar economic order based upon stable exchange rates and a recognition that economic autarky was a threat to peace and democratic governments. At Dumbarton Oaks and the San Francisco Conference, a charter was drawn up for a new United Nations, seeking to avoid the limitations of its predecessor, the League of Nations. Stalin refused to join the United Nations unless the members of the Security Council were granted veto power – a concession that brought the Cold War great power competition into the United Nations and complicated its efforts to peacefully resolve interstate conflicts.

For both the US and USSR, the immediate postwar years prompted significant reassessment. The post-war world largely subverted Soviet expectations. Instead of renewing the imperialist conflict over colonies, resources, and markets that Marxist-Leninist theory claimed had already produced two world wars, the capitalist powers remained united and embarked upon unprecedented political, economic, and military cooperation. Moreover, instead of returning to a state of economic depression, the West enjoyed an economic boom that lasted until the 1970s.

The Soviets’ flawed assessment exacerbated the communist regime’s fear and paranoia and caused it to reject any cooperation with the West. These actions prompted the first US reassessment of its postwar strategy in 1946, producing George Kennan’s vision of containment.

For Kennan, containment was a simple strategy that required U.S. leaders abandon their fruitless policy of negotiating with a Soviet regime that was, by ideological conviction, uninterested in peace with the capitalist world. Kennan did not advocate confrontation – U.S. military action was only required for the preservation of key regions including the U.S. homeland, Western Europe, Japan, and the Middle East for its oil resources. Otherwise, Americans need only keep the USSR from expanding and allow communism to collapse of its own accord. The 1947 Truman Doctrine with its defense of Greek democracy against communist insurgency was the initial instantiation of containment in US policy.

U.S. plans quickly ran into economic difficulties. The nations of Europe lacked the foreign exchange to pay for their reconstruction, and drought plus a harsh winter (1946-1947) wiped out their remaining supplies and savings. U.S. officials realized that hungry people trying to rebuild their lives amidst unprecedented destruction might turn towards communism as a potential solution to their problems. Rebuilding European industry so that they might earn dollars through exports and become dependable US trading partners proved nearly impossible given the level of destruction wrought by the war. Fixing the issues required the 1948 Marshall Plan, an unprecedented display of generosity in which the US subsidized its allies with massive infusions of both dollars and goods. Buoyed by U.S. taxpayer dollars, the American bases in Europe— where GIs spent freely and Europeans found steady employment—gradually helped close the “dollar gap,” the difference between what Europeans possessed and what they needed to buy U.S.-made goods. The gap’s legacy would continue shaping American perceptions of Europe long after it disappeared.

By 1948, the Soviets finished crushing the non-communist governments within their sphere and blockaded access to West Berlin. The following year, the communists triumphed in the Chinese Civil War, and the Soviet Union detonated its first atomic bomb years before the United States expected it. War now became a distinct possibility, but the United States was unprepared. President Truman’s fixation on cutting the budget, combined with bitter interservice rivalry, poisoned civil-military relations. Congress was hostile to extending further economic assistance to Europe, much less Japan, which had not received aid under the Marshall Plan.

Kennan had no answer to these problems and lost the confidence of the Truman Administration. Paul Nitze, his replacement at the State Department, gave containment teeth. For Nitze, the Soviet Union was an active threat driven by an ideological imperative to expand across Western Europe and the Eurasian landmass. Nitze and his colleagues in the State Department and Pentagon crafted NSC-68 as a response to Kennan. This remarkable document, the product of a committee rather than an individual, argued that the best course of action for the U.S. was a massive military buildup of both nuclear and conventional forces, including the yet unproven

hydrogen or thermonuclear bomb. Such military expansion would eliminate the hope for a balanced U.S. budget but would promote economic expansion through the stimulus of government spending, as occurred during World War II. The resulting economic growth would shrink the debt as a percentage of the overall economy, even if the deficit grew. President Truman, who held distinctly old-fashioned views about public finance and distrusted the military to spend money wisely, originally shelved NSC-68 and made only modest spending increases.

Among the preconditions for NSC-68 was the August 1949 Soviet detonation of an atomic bomb. The end of the American nuclear monopoly yielded a profound change in the American polity, one in which it became clear that technology was not solely an American advantage. In turn, NSC-68 posited that the development of thermonuclear weapons, a leap forward in destructive power, would become essential to the arsenals of both nations. For the USSR, the rapid assembly and demonstration of a nuclear weapon demonstrated the power of the Soviet system as well as the nation’s potential equality with the US. For Americans, the Soviet detonation accelerated a domestic Red Scare. At the same time, NSC-68’s militarization rekindled fears that the US might become a “garrison state” in which specialists on violence would become the dominant force.

Ironically, despite believing in the centrality of Europe for the future of the U.S., it was in Asia where the Cold War became hot. Stalin approved Kim Il Sung’s plans for a North Korean invasion of South Korea, which took place in June 1950. The Korean War, which occurred far from the Cold War’s ostensibly central front along the Iron Curtain, ended the first phase of the U.S.-Soviet Cold War. Still, not even the outbreak of the Korean War convinced Truman to put the U.S. on a permanent war footing, since General Douglas MacArthur’s brilliant landing at Inchon in September suggested that the war would end by Christmas. However, China’s massive military intervention on behalf of North Korea in November 1950 dramatically transformed the Korean War into a dangerous regional and potentially global conflict.

This case also highlights the fragile nature of civil-military relations. General MacArthur repeatedly clashed with President Truman by advocating for escalations, including nuclear strikes and deploying Nationalist troops. When Truman rejected these strategies, MacArthur publicly criticized him, leading to his dismissal for insubordination. Ultimately, this rift exposed the difficulties of maintaining civilian control during wartime.

The Korean War marked a pivotal shift in American Cold War strategy. Shaped by Paul Nitze’s stark assessment of Soviet ambitions in NSC-68, U.S. foreign policy expanded globally to confront of the Soviet Union and communist China. To contain communism in Asia, Washington positioned Japan as a key regional bulwark. Furthermore, the Korean War prompted the United States to extend its security network beyond Europe and NATO. Through regional pacts such as the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO) and the Australia, New Zealand, United States Security Treaty (ANZUS), the US cemented its role as the global guarantor against communist expansion. Yet, this global treaty network often blinded Washington to the fact that the Korean conflict was not just a Cold War chessboard, but a violent byproduct of the messy decolonization process reshaping the post-war world.

Case VIIIMiddle Cold WarLectures Jan 28, 29 · Seminars Feb 2, 3Case study
The Middle Cold War: U.S., Soviet, and Chinese Competition in the Third World, 1953–1968

Overview

The second case study in the Cold War trilogy examines the intersection of decolonization and superpower rivalry, including the war in Vietnam and a near-war over Cuba.

While in the early Cold War study students examined the Winning the Peace and Preparing for War course theme, this case study will cover Interaction, Adaptation, and Reassessment, and Civil-Military Relations and the Institutional Dimensions of Strategy. These course themes allow students to evaluate how war termination in Korea and in the First Indochina War reshaped the policies and strategies of the major belligerents. How did the influence of the great powers – the United States, PRC, and USSR – interact with the situation on the battlefield and the objectives of the Koreans and Vietnamese to shape the outcomes of these wars? What lessons did the great powers learn based on their involvement in these wars, and how did they reassess their political objectives and strategies in the aftermath? Finally, how did tensions between civilian and military leaders shape this era, and what lessons can be drawn regarding the impact of civil–military relations on strategic success?

Read the full case overview (26 more paragraphs)

For the United States, the war in Korea had been a peripheral operation suitable for a maritime power, but for the communist adversaries, it was a land war to protect their continental borders. The Chinese paid a heavy price in blood and treasure while Joseph Stalin sought to protract the war at minimal cost to the USSR. Negotiations over war termination stalled until the death of Joseph Stalin in March 1953 removed the one insuperable obstacle to reaching an agreement. In 1954, the long war for the fate of Indochina between France and Ho Chi Minh’s Vietnamese communists was decided in Geneva, and the resulting divided Vietnam reflected the interests of the Americans, Soviets, and Chinese more so than it did the battlefield realities on the ground. It also laid the foundation for the subsequent American war in Vietnam. Students should evaluate the conditions under which war termination leads to a “better state of peace.”

In the Soviet Union, Nikita Khrushchev won a brief power struggle after Stalin’s death.

His denunciation of Stalin at the 20th Party Congress in 1956 stunned fellow communists and ushered in a period of instability in the “bloc,” as Soviet bloc states grappled with the consequences of Khrushchev’s disclosure of Stalin’s crimes. Mao Zedong and the Chinese communists were furious about the so-called “Secret Speech,” and feared the consequences of de-Stalinization for their own rule in China. Students should examine leadership styles in the communist world and the political and strategic implications of different personalities. To what extent did Sino-Soviet hostilities reflect the personalities of Mao and Khrushchev? To what extent was the Sino-Soviet rivalry an ideological contest over competing theories of revolution, and to what extent was it grounded in geopolitical realities?

The 1957 launch of the Sputnik satellite provoked a serious reassessment of U.S. scientific and technological capabilities. Scientific research became an urgent national security concern, as Americans assumed that the success of Sputnik demonstrated Soviet technological superiority. American intelligence estimates of Soviet nuclear capabilities were wildly off base, ranging upwards of hundreds of inter-continental ballistic missiles at a time when the USSR only had four ICBMs (as would later become clear with U2 overflights of Soviet territory).

Khrushchev’s own bellicose rhetoric, claiming that Soviet factories were pumping out missiles “like sausages,” only compounded US fears, as did his willingness to pursue nuclear

brinkmanship in the Suez crisis. John F. Kennedy campaigned for the presidency on the issue of this supposed “missile gap” between the US and USSR. Once in the White House, Kennedy quickly learned that the missile gap was a myth, or rather, that there was a missile gap, but it was dramatically in the U.S.’s favor. Nevertheless, he continued with the defense buildup as planned.

Students should examine the Civil-Military Relations and Institutional Dimension of Strategy course theme to understand why this course of action was pursued even as the intelligence estimates were shown to be wildly exaggerated.

This case study offers an excellent opportunity to examine the Instruments of National Power in the nuclear age. During the Eisenhower Presidency, the administration tried to rein in defense spending by relying on two extreme instruments for coercion. The first was the use of covert action, which seemed to pay off in 1953 and 1954, when CIA-backed coups toppled leaders in Iran and Guatemala pursuing policies contrary to U.S. interests. The second was the doctrine of “massive retaliation,” whereby the United States promised to respond to future provocations such as the Korean War not by a steady escalation of force but rather the application of overwhelming violence at the very start of a conflict – up to and including the use of thermonuclear weapons.

American defense analysts devised nuclear policy and doctrine with the aim of deterrence, and their debates reflected uncertainty about the USSR’s nuclear arsenal and strategic thinking. The strategic dilemma of Berlin was at the center of these debates. While the United States sought to protect West Germany and West Berlin from Soviet aggression, the Soviets desperately sought to stanch the flow of migration from East to West Berlin, and the construction of the Berlin Wall in 1961 served as the most literal manifestation of the “Iron Curtain.” The Kennedy administration’s search for a “flexible response” was a reaction to frustration with “massive retaliation,” which many U.S. military and civilian defense analysts did not consider a realistic strategy, particularly as the Soviet thermonuclear arsenal grew. One option was to build up conventional forces in Western Europe to deter a Warsaw Pact invasion, but this would have exacerbated the US balance-of-payments deficit, potentially leading to unsustainable US dollar and gold outflows under the Bretton Woods system, which pegged the dollar to gold.

While US policymakers and strategists continued to view Europe as the primary theater of the Cold War, events elsewhere kept drawing their attention back to the Third World. In 1961, Khrushchev delivered a speech lauding “wars of national liberation” in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Although his message was likely aimed just as much at the Chinese as it was at the Americans, the Washington defense establishment viewed it as a direct challenge. The Eisenhower and Kennedy administrations embraced covert operations as a means of containing communism in the Third World on the cheap. Students should evaluate whether the short-term success of these operations led to long-term stability, or whether they ultimately undermined US and global security. With each successive wave of decolonization, the US-USSR-PRC rivalry added an international and ideological dimension to what might have otherwise been isolated civil wars in Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

The 1959 Cuban revolution and the development of the Cuban-Soviet alliance confronted the United States with a direct challenge inside the Western Hemisphere, long considered Washington’s exclusive sphere of influence. As Fidel Castro and Ernesto “Che” Guevara consolidated the revolution and tacked to the left, they sought the support of the Soviet Union as

the only great power capable of protecting their regime against US hostility. The radicalization of the Cuban revolution drove many of Castro’s former comrades into opposition, many of whom migrated to the U.S. The Cuban exile community in Miami became a powerful domestic political constituency, and the CIA sponsored an exile-led invasion at the Bay of Pigs in 1961.

The operation was flawed from the outset, and President Kennedy’s refusal to supply air support doomed the exiles to capture or worse. In the aftermath, the Cuban population rallied around the regime, and the United States suffered a blow to its reputation as the leader of the free world.

The most profound consequence of the failed Bay of Pigs invasion, however, was Khrushchev’s decision to station nuclear missiles in Cuba.

The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the world has ever come to nuclear annihilation.

The crisis pitted Khrushchev against Kennedy, Khrushchev against Castro, and Kennedy against his own advisers, particularly the generals. This offers students an excellent opportunity to examine alliance dynamics between great powers and smaller states, as Khrushchev pursued negotiations with Kennedy without advancing Cuban demands or even bothering to inform Castro. Moreover, it provides students with a window into civil-military relations in the United States, and the implications for nuclear policy and doctrine of these institutional arrangements.

The outcome of the crisis, despite forcing the United States to tolerate the existence of the Cuban revolution, ultimately deepened the rift dividing the PRC and USSR.

Concurrent with the Cuban Missile Crisis was the Sino-Indian War of 1962, a dramatic escalation of border skirmishes that had begun after the 1959 uprising in Tibet. China and India had both attended the 1955 Afro-Asian Solidarity Conference in Bandung, Indonesia, and had declared their support for the conference’s foundational principles of peaceful coexistence, nonaggression, and respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty. In practice, it proved difficult for decolonizing states to operationalize these principles, as the former European colonial powers had drawn borders often with the express intent to “divide and conquer.” Postcolonial states proved resistant to redrawing those borders, and despite rhetorical fealty to the “spirit of Bandung,” the primacy of national interests and the heightened appeal of nationalism frequently took precedence over Third World solidarity.

The International Dimension theme demonstrates the impact of geography and coalitions on wars and competitions. Geography challenged the United States and its allies by affording logistical and sanctuary advantages to the North Vietnamese communists. The making and breaking of coalitions played a major role in determining the outcome of the wars in Southeast Asia. Communist alliances alternately restrained Hanoi and bolstered its firepower, while the United States faced international challenges around the globe even as it fought to contain communism in Southeast Asia. The breakdown of the partnership between Saigon and Washington paved the way for the communist victory in Vietnam. Students will examine how the Nixon administration was able to “operationalize” the Sino-Soviet split through triangular diplomacy, transforming the international environment with significant consequences for the Second and Third Indochina Wars.

The Cultural and Social Dimensions of Strategy theme plays an important role during this period. How important was ideology in explaining great-power rivalry and competition during the Cold War? Did shared ideology strengthen the Sino-Soviet partnership or weaken it? How

did domestic politics shape US, Soviet, and Chinese foreign policy? How did cultural values and social norms influence the development of policy and strategy?

The case study also introduces civil-military relations theory. This theory examines how interactions among civilian authorities, military professionals, and society shape the formulation and execution of strategy, influence strategic adaptability, and condition political support in war.

Understanding the responsibilities of political and military leaders for the formulation of strategy and its implementation in the theater remains a critical requirement for national security professionals.

While the last case dealt with Mao as a political actor, this case adds Mao as a theorist of insurgency and unconventional warfare. Well before World War II, he produced a series of writings on how weaker actors can use irregular warfare and protraction as tools to victory.

During the Cold War and since, revolutionaries throughout the developing world have seized on his ideas and used them as weapons against imperial powers and domestic regimes alike.

Governments in power were forced to devise ways to counter and defeat Maoist-style insurgencies, with mixed success.

Case IXLate Cold WarLectures Feb 4, 5 · Seminars Feb 8, 9Case study
The Late Cold War: From Détente to the Collapse of the Soviet Union, 1969–1991

Overview

This case study examines how the Cold War ended. Beginning with détente, a general reduction in tension between the U.S. and the Soviet Union in the late 1960s and early 1970s, it continues into the collapse of détente and a renewal of rivalry under the presidency of Ronald Reagan. It then turns to the Soviet Union’s major internal and external challenges in the 1980s, and then final end of the Cold War, followed by the disintegration of the Soviet Union. It asks why the Cold War, unlike many other great power rivalries, ended relatively peacefully. Did the United States triumph because of a patient U.S. strategy of containment? Or do Soviet structural problems and ineffective leadership explain the outcome? In other words, do the choices of individual actors’ matter, or are they outweighed by power imbalances, structural weaknesses, and ideological blinders?

This case begins as the United States sought to reduce tension with the Soviets and extricate itself from its disastrous intervention in Vietnam. As its architects in the United States explained, détente aimed not to end the Cold War but to stabilize it. Détente envisioned a “structure of peace” whereby great powers would compete peacefully to gain from cooperation rather than confrontation. From the U.S. perspective, détente meant normalizing relations with the communist nations in exchange for concessions on key national interests. Through the application of sticks and carrots, the United States hoped to reduce Soviet and Chinese support for North Vietnam, thereby allowing the United States to withdraw from South Vietnam.

Read the full case overview (12 more paragraphs)

Every nation had its own rationale for embracing détente. Lyndon Johnson’s administration tried in vain to induce the Soviet Union and China to help the United States withdraw from Vietnam. After 1969, however, the situation changed. First, the Federal Republic of Germany decided to normalize its relations with its eastern neighbors. Unless the U.S. joined this process, it risked becoming isolated within its own alliance. More importantly, divisions within the communist world made détente possible. In 1969, the Soviet Union and People’s Republic of China had a border clash that almost escalated into all-out war. The Soviet Union was so concerned with the Chinese threat that, according to one of Henry Kissinger’s aides, the Soviet Union floated the idea of the United States standing by while the Soviet Union obliterated China’s nuclear-weapons facilities. The U.S. rejected this proposal, seeing in the confrontation an opportunity. Through a process of “triangular diplomacy,” the U.S. tried to play its two foes against one another for diplomatic gains. Since the U.S. had to restrain defense spending in the wake of Vietnam, arms control--managing the growth in U.S. and Soviet nuclear arsenals--soon became an emphasis of détente. The Soviet Union had long sought equality of status with the United States. It was willing to make accommodations in exchange for recognition of the postWorld War II status quo in Eastern Europe and greater access to Western markets and technology. China meanwhile needed to end its diplomatic isolation in the wake of the SinoSoviet rift. U.S. support for China might dissuade the Soviet Union from taking military action against China.

Détente unraveled in the second half of the 1970s. The United States hoped to enforce détente through “linkage”: U.S. concessions in one sphere would be matched by the Soviet Union in another. Unfortunately, the Soviet Union’s clients did not simply heed the guidance of

their patron and instead acted in their own self-interest, undermining U.S. interests. The beginning of the end came in Angola in 1975, where the Soviets supported the deployment of Cuban troops to counter forces backed by the United States, South Africa, France, and China.

From the U.S. perspective, détente looked like a one-way street that enabled Communist expansion. The Soviets also soured on détente once the promised gains from greater economic trade evaporated. The 1974 Jackson-Vanik Amendment punished the Soviet regime for not allowing the emigration of Soviet Jews by withholding most-favored-nation trading status. This legislation signified the growing importance of human rights in international affairs, which complicated the operation of détente. Finally, Mao Zedong was furious that the United States had exploited China to seek an accommodation with Moscow.

Between 1975 and 1989, a remarkable turnaround occurred in American fortunes. In 1975, Saigon fell to North Vietnamese forces. During the final communist offensive, the United States refused to offer air support for South Vietnam. Domestically, the U.S. suffered from inflation arising from the collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary system in 1971 and the energy crisis starting in 1973. U.S. foreign policy was hamstrung by stagflation and the Watergate scandal. The Soviet Union, bolstered by oil revenues, was seemingly on the march, especially in the Third World. Meanwhile, Third World countries nationalized key industries such as oil upon which the developed West depended. The collapse of the Shah’s regime in Iran was an especially grievous blow, as Iran was a major oil producer, and the U.S. relied on it to safeguard Western interests in the Middle East. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 not only seemed to presage a Soviet move against the Middle East but also signaled the final collapse of détente.

Finally, the military balance seemed to shift decisively in favor of the Soviet Union, which in the 1970s added parity in strategic (nuclear) weapons to its quantitative superiority in conventional weapons. Strategic parity, U.S. hawks warned, would allow the Soviet Union to coerce U.S. allies in Europe and Asia since they lacked the ability to match the Soviet Union in conventional forces and risked total destruction if they used nuclear weapons, which had always been central to NATO’s military strategy.

By 1989, the situation had completely reversed. The United States, through harsh monetary policies, managed to end stagflation. The information-technology revolution transformed the economy of the West. These new technologies combined with the largest peacetime defense budgets in American history to shift the military balance back in favor of the U.S. and its allies. On the other side of the Iron Curtain, the Soviet Union’s economy staggered under the dual shocks of declining oil prices and a moribund economy. Efforts to reform the economy only heightened its dysfunction, and Communist leaders’ growing dependence on the West discouraged them from using force to repress opposition groups in Eastern Europe. The Soviet Union was also bogged down fighting or supporting costly guerilla wars in Southeast Asia, Africa, Central America, and Afghanistan. China, which had warmed further to the United States following Mao’s death in 1976, even supported the U.S. assistance program to the Afghan guerrillas fighting the Soviet-backed regime. Finally, the U.S. advantage in the “revolution in military affairs” convinced Soviet military leaders that they could not win a conventional war against NATO and enabled moderate Soviet leaders to press for arms reduction and dismantling the Soviet empire.

How should national-security practitioners understand the reversal in U.S. fortunes between the fall of Saigon and the end of the Cold War? Was the United States stronger in the 1970s, and the Soviet Union weaker, than they appeared? Or was it a validation of President Ronald Reagan’s rejection of détente – indeed of containment as a whole – and his stated policy of rolling back Soviet gains? Reagan told his chief foreign policy adviser in 1978 that his strategy for the Cold War was simple: “We win, they lose.” Reagan was the beneficiary of excellent timing. His administration capitalized on investments, particularly during the Carter Administration, that yielded improved military capabilities. By the mid-1980s, the United States and its allies in Europe and the Pacific were now confident that they could successfully repel a Soviet military offensive through conventional means and strike at the Soviet Union on its flanks with naval forces. Reagan and his team were convinced that the U.S. defense buildup would persuade the Soviet Union that it could not compete and must negotiate an end to the Cold War. Although the U.S. military was focused on warfighting (to the point where the U.S. Navy adopted a Maritime Strategy that explicitly undermined the Soviet Union’s nuclear deterrent) the Reagan Administration always considered its strategy of “pressure” to be an inducement to negotiation.

Reagan himself understood that his confrontational rhetoric had gone too far in late 1983, when for several weeks the Soviet leadership wondered if the United States was considering a surprise nuclear attack. Reagan, with access to high-level intelligence on the thinking of Soviet leaders, reacted by offering to return to diplomacy. Reagan’s greatest contribution to ending the Cold War was perhaps enabling the actions of the man who inadvertently destroyed the Soviet Union: Mikhail Gorbachev. He knew full well the extent of Soviet economic dysfunction. The dynamism of the Soviet economy had peaked in the 1960s, but the country failed to make the transition from extensive to intensive economic growth. In other words, the Soviet economy produced large amounts of industrial goods, but productivity stalled, meaning that Soviet production remained inefficient. Additionally, the Soviet Union failed to develop light industry, meaning that living standards, and therefore productivity, remained low. The gradual decline of oil prices in the 1980s removed an important support of the Soviet system. Gorbachev wanted to revolutionize the Soviet economic system by delegating authority downward to state-owned firms, but he risked undermining the legitimacy of the Soviet regime if he failed. Conservatives had blocked would-be reformers in the past by pointing to the U.S. threat. Gorbachev could overcome this resistance by using Reagan’s determination to end the Cold War by negotiation.

Students should address the issues surrounding war termination and examine how they apply to the Cold War. Did the United States create a “better state of peace,” or was there more that U.S. leaders could have done to effectively incorporate the defeated powers (Russia, the former Soviet states, and Eastern Europe) into the international system? Finally, did the United States end up creating a more-formidable foe in China by building it up as a counterweight to the Soviet Union? The Reagan Administration, and the U.S. Navy in particular, was a keen advocate of transferring advanced military technology to China to draw Soviet forces away from Europe.

The Joint Chiefs of Staff warned that these transfers would have little immediate impact on the military balance but would have profound long-term consequences by undermining U.S. Melvyn P. The Global War on Terrorism: Multi-Theater Warfare Against State and Non-State Adversaries

Overview

The Global War on Terror (GWOT) exposed the friction that occurs when a conventional superpower confronts a decentralized, transnational non-state adversary. Since its emergence in the 1990s, Al-Qaeda’s stated objectives were global, and it succeeded in conducting spectacular acts of terrorism using minimal military capabilities. Among its primary objectives was to eject Western forces from the Middle East, topple apostate or corrupt governments, and establish a supranational caliphate. Al-Qaeda successfully employed an asymmetric strategy which culminated in the devastating September 11 attacks. In response, the United States mobilized its massive defense bureaucracy, restructured its domestic security apparatus, and launched multi-theater regional wars that led to more than two decades of fighting terrorism across much of the globe. This case explores this complex era of asymmetric warfare that fundamentally tested the utility of conventional military power against an adaptive, ideologically driven network.

The following course themes provide useful tools for examining the critical divide between operational success and strategic victory in this case: the interrelationship among policy, strategy, and operations; interaction, adaptation, and reassessment; and war termination. In two major theaters, U.S. forces dismantled the adversary’s physical command hubs but struggled to disrupt Al-Qaeda’s true Clausewitzian center of gravity: its resilient and amorphous ideology. By contrast, Al-Qaeda followed Sun Tzu’s maxim to “avoid what is strong and strike at what is weak,” using low-cost, high-impact terrorism to draw a superpower designed for peer conflict into prolonged economic, political, and military commitments. Over time, the U.S. military adapted by targeting the network’s financing, exploiting internal organizational fractures, and relentlessly hunting and eliminating key leaders. This evolution reflected tactical and intelligence flexibility, even as U.S. grand strategy remained constrained by institutional inertia and the limits of a conventional warfare mindset.

Two main theaters in the GWOT exemplified the complexities of multi-theater operations. In both Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003), rapid tactical victories on the part of the U.S. forces clashed with the political realities within these two vastly different nations, directly violating Clausewitz's foundational principle that war is a continuation of politics by other means. While the U.S. was able to quickly dismantle the ruling regimes in both Iraq and Afghanistan, these successes created chaotic security vacuums that inadvertently spawned protracted insurgencies and forced a U.S. military built for short-duration operations into costly, population-centric counterinsurgency campaigns. In Afghanistan, the core U.S. objectives began with targeted retribution against al-Qaeda and morphed into a more ambitious nation-building mandate that sought to graft a centralized, Western-style democracy onto a historically fragmented, tribal society. U.S. efforts were undercut because the Taliban used safe havens across the border in Pakistan to regroup and refit.

Similar structural failures and a flawed net assessment also undermined the U.S. intervention in Iraq in 2003. After the invasion, the United States adopted policies that weakened its original objectives. The most consequential were de-Ba'athification and the abrupt dissolution of the Iraqi Army, which destroyed the state’s security backbone and fueled a virulent sectarian

insurgency that persisted for years. U.S. planners failed to adjust their state-building objectives and instead engaged in mirror-imaging, assuming Iraqis would prioritize democratic institutionbuilding over deeply rooted tribal and sectarian identities. This failure to heed Sun Tzu’s dictum to “know your enemy” weakened reassessment efforts and prolonged war termination. Some argue that the United States successfully reassessed and adapted during the 2007 “Surge” by exploiting local tribal fractures to weaken insurgents and reduce casualties. Yet this tactical reset proved temporary and failed to achieve its central objective: lasting political reconciliation between Shia and Sunni factions.

This case provides students with an opportunity to examine how a conflict that began with clear objectives following 9/11 evolved into a two decade-long conflict in multiple theaters.

The specific events in this case reshaped regional dynamics and stability and set in motion a profound change in the trajectory of U.S. global influence. This case encourages critical discussions about if and how multi-theater warfare can best be conducted when the adversary employs asymmetric and unconventional strategies. Moreover, it also raises important questions that shape today’s geopolitical environment such as whether the vacuum left in Iraq following the overthrow of Saddam Hussein led directly to the ascendance of Iran as a regional hegemon?

With respect to incomplete war termination in the Afghan theater, how did the eventual transition of power in Afghanistan degrade U.S. credibility, and what unique strategic opportunities or challenges did it offer to China and Russia? Finally, did the expenditure of trillions of dollars during these multi-decade campaigns divert critical resources away from the strategic pivot to the Indo-Pacific?

Essay & Discussion Questions (20)

  1. Were the September 11 attacks a good or bad strategic choice for al-Qaeda?
  2. Clausewitz stresses that the primary task of political and military leaders is to understand the nature of the war in which they are engaging. What strategic implications derive from an assessment of the nature of this war and how did it change over time?
  3. How did the “value of the Object” shape the decisions of U.S. leaders in the war against al Qaeda?
  4. Which belligerent—the United States and its allies or al-Qaeda/ISIS —did a better job of adapting and reassessing during the period covered by this case?
  5. In what ways does the ideological competition of the War on Terror differ from the ideological competition of the Cold War?
  6. Did it make strategic sense for the United States to engage in a multi-theater war during the War on Terror?
  7. Was the 2007 Iraq ‘Surge’ framework exportable to counterinsurgency strategy in Afghanistan?
  8. Which best explains the U.S. inability to defeat its enemy in Vietnam and Afghanistan: the failure of political leaders to produce clear and achievable political aims or the failure of military leaders to implement adequate strategies to defeat the enemy?
  9. Were there realistic opportunities for war termination during any of the phases of this conflict? If so, when, and how? If not, why not?
  10. Why did the United States and its allies find it difficult to achieve their objectives in Afghanistan and Iraq?
  11. Were there realistic opportunities for war termination at any juncture during this long conflict? If so, when, and how? If not, why not?
  12. In what ways have culture and religion shaped American involvement in the Middle East and how does this compare with other case studies in this course?
  13. Drawing on this case and others in the course, what conditions compel an adversary to reassess and adapt its strategic priorities when engaged in a long-term competition?
  14. Drawing on this case and others in the course, under what conditions should a hegemonic power close a major theater of operation?
  15. Did the United States achieve “a better state of peace” in the War on Terror?
  16. How did elements of “strategic culture” shape the ways in which U.S. leaders approached the problem of counterterrorism and counterinsurgency?
  17. In what ways has two decades of counterinsurgency impacted U.S. preparedness for the current era of Great Power Competition.
  18. What lessons can be drawn by comparing the war against violent extremism described in this case study to wars against non-state actors in other case studies?
  19. Using examples from previous case studies, what mix of military and diplomatic action could have produced either a favorable or acceptable outcome in the Afghanistan theater for the U.S. and its allies.
  20. What key lessons on the impact of culture and the role of non-state actors can contemporary leaders draw from the U.S. experience in this case study?

Global Challenges: The Future of American Power and the China Challenge

Overview

The case reviews contemporary challenges through the lenses of the current National Security Strategy and National Defense Strategy, which rank theaters of interest as (1) Western Hemisphere, (2) Indo-Pacific, (3) Europe, (4) Middle East, and (5) everywhere else. Of note, these directives essentially treat the Indo-Pacific as indivisible from Western Hemisphere prosperity and security.

This closing case gives us one final opportunity to bridge back through time for lessons of the past while applying strategic theory and course themes to the present. This helps prepare students for the final exam and cement habits of mind that will serve Naval War College graduates well in their future endeavors. The case asks students to survey world affairs and critique U.S. foreign policy and strategy in an age of geopolitical and geoeconomic competition.

Read the full case overview (17 more paragraphs)

Some overarching questions can help frame this tour d’horizon. What are America’s purposes in the world? What are the most important security challenges confronting U.S. leaders? Are we witnessing the emergence of a post-American world where the United States is no longer the leading global power? Is major-power war unthinkable, or will arms competitions among the great powers increase the probability of war? How is technological innovation driving the quest for security in the international system? How can the United States deter war and curtail violence in international affairs? Does the United States have sufficient reserves of power to meet challenges posed by international rivals and compete effectively against them?

An assessment of U.S. foreign-policy choices and strategic priorities requires us to examine the sources of power sustaining the United States’ international position. Since the end of the Cold War, the United States has conducted military campaigns throughout the greater Middle East. Yet an unsettled Western Hemisphere, the growth of China’s power, and Russia’s turn to aggression have prompted U.S. leaders to reassess their strategic priorities. The war on terror has given way on the list of priorities to efforts to manage competition among great powers and deter conflicts that could upend the international order that has existed since 1945.

The motivations for reshuffling priorities are clear. The Donald J. Trump administration places great stock in managing events in the Western Hemisphere. Counterbalancing the People’s Republic of China ranks as a close second priority in security and defense directives, followed by the European and Middle East theaters. The administration regards the Western Hemisphere as a longstanding zone of neglect and has vowed to reassert U.S. dominance under the “Trump Corollary,” a revival of the “Theodore Roosevelt Corollary” to the Monroe Doctrine, the foreignpolicy precept whereby the United States forbade European empires from reasserting control over former Latin American colonies and interfering in the affairs of the Americas. . Given that the U.S. government disavowed the Roosevelt Corollary a century ago, its comeback marks a striking turnaround in U.S. foreign policy and strategy. Interestingly, the Trump Corollary concerns itself with constabulary matters such as counternarcotics and immigration as well as the traditional great-power politics that motivated the Roosevelt Corollary. The National Security Strategy and National Defense Strategy mark a revolution – or perhaps a counterrevolution – in American strategy and policy.

Turning to Eurasia, the traditional focal point for U.S. policy and strategy, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine spelled an end to the relative calm that Europe enjoyed after the Cold War.

Vladimir Putin’s decision in 2022 to launch a major war in Europe inaugurated the largest and deadliest conflict in Europe since 1945, shocking leaders in Europe and around the world. Many liberal democracies have rallied behind Ukraine, underscoring the burgeoning clash between defenders of the U.S.-led world order and the authoritarian great powers such as Russia, China, and Iran that are intent on overturning it. Deterring an even larger war in Europe and containing Russian power have become crucial aims for the United States.

The peace in Asia also appears increasingly precarious, endangered by Chinese ambitions and military might. While Russia’s assault on Ukraine has focused attention on Europe, American leaders view China as the greatest challenge to the liberal world order. China’s rulers might use their nation’s growing strength to seize Taiwan during the coming decade. By staging military exercises of increasing sweep, China has demonstrated the capability of its armed forces to project power in the Western Pacific. An assault on Taiwan would compel the United States and its major partners – especially Japan, Australia, and South Korea, its principal allies – to choose whether and how to resist Chinee aggression. How to deter such a war ranks foremost in the minds of U.S. and allied leaders. In examining the challenges faced by the United States, this final case study thus pays particular attention to the threat posed by China’s ambition to revise the U.S.-led international order.

In examining the challenge posed by China in particular, it is useful to recall Thucydides’ claim that honor, fear, and interest are three of the most powerful motives goading competitors to go to war. These three impulses have driven China to acquire greater capabilities to wage war, while fear of China’s ambitions and capabilities has prompted the United States and its partners to respond in kind. Rising powers such as Athens, Napoleonic France, imperial and Nazi Germany, the United States, the Soviet Union, and China figure prominently in the Strategy and Policy Course. Why has the rise of some powers – but not others – culminated in a war? As past rising powers have done, could China launch a war yet miscalculate how America responds to aggression? Or will China seek to win without fighting in the tradition of Sun Tzu? Are there actions the United States can take to deter China from using force, or does the decision for war reside in Beijing? Could allies or coalition partners spark a maritime war between the United States and China – much as fighting between Corinth and Corcyra escalated into wholescale conflict between Athens and Sparta?

In many ways the rise of China as a sea power resembles the rivalries Mahan recounted in his classic books. In a 2018 speech during China’s largest display of maritime power ever, General Secretary of the Communist Party of China Xi Jinping exhorted his country to construct a world-class navy. As Xi spoke, forty-eight surface vessels and submarines steamed across the South China Sea, including the aircraft carrier Liaoning, while scores of aircraft streaked overhead. He told the assembled officers and crews that there had never been a more pressing need for a powerful Chinese navy.

This display of naval might fanned Chinese nationalism while rallying support for the regime. Xi’s speech calls to mind past appeals to national greatness from rising naval powers. At

the turn of the twentieth century, Kaiser Wilhelm II insisted that his country, historically a land power, urgently needed a larger navy to challenge Britain. The Kaiser saw a formidable navy as a token of Germany’s standing in the international arena and a way to unite the German people behind a national endeavor. However, Germany’s naval buildup challenged Britain’s position as the world’s leading sea power. The antagonism inflamed by the Anglo-German rivalry at sea sent a strong undercurrent propelling Germany and Britain toward war. The World War I case study, in other words, sends a warning from the past of the dangers that follow when great sea powers struggle for mastery of the maritime commons and when traditional continental powers attempt to project power into the maritime domain.

Thinking about U.S.-China relations requires understanding the strengths and limitations of all instruments of national power possessed by both countries. Sea powers typically must integrate multiple elements of national power – most notably trade, finance, diplomacy, and military and economic aid. Like Britain and Japan, China depends on food and energy imports.

What part will these dependencies play in a competition among the great powers, and more importantly during war? Any US. -China conflict will have global ramifications. How will U.S. allies and enemies respond? And what are the strategic dangers posed by a partnership between Xi’s China and Putin’s Russia? How can strategic planners develop strategies for global force management to meet threats to the security of the United States and partner countries?

It is vital that political leaders, national security professionals, and strategic planners try to foresee not only how a war might start, but how it might end. What courses of action might deliver desired political objectives at a cost and risk commensurate with the value of the object?

What actions would produce a better state of peace, and what actions might make things worse?

Of particular importance is the threat of nuclear escalation in a U.S.-China conflict. How might a naval conflict escalate into conventional or even nuclear attacks on each country’s homeland?

Great-power war will also see the use of advanced technologies that promise to shape the course and outcome of fighting. How can the examples of past technological innovations and transformations in warfare inform decisions about strategy? What roles will data-driven technologies, artificial intelligence, machine learning, and cyberspace play in a conflict, and which side will derive more competitive advantage from employing them?

Decisions to escalate the fighting demand rigorous moral and ethical questioning as part of strategic deliberations. These considerations attest to the wisdom of Sun Tzu’s opening lines: “War is a matter of vital importance to the State; the province of life and death; the road to survival and ruin. It is mandatory that it be thoroughly studied.” This past term we have followed Sun Tzu’s mandate to study war – its origins, its conduct, and its consequences – and to master the skills of critical strategic analysis. These skills have never been needed more.

Essay & Discussion Questions (20)

  1. What are the main sources of American strength and weakness in competitions with international rivals?
  2. Is the United States in retreat and decline as a world power? If so, what does it mean for the security and well-being of the American people and for its allies? If the United States is not in decline, why is that the case?
  3. What insights can the study of Thucydides provide about the nature of contests between democracies and authoritarian regimes?
  4. Before going to war, Pericles, the Athenian leader, and the Spartan king Archidamus provided net assessments about the wisdom of fighting. What would national security professionals present to an American President as the main elements of a net assessment involving a contest between China and the United States? What would Chinese strategic analysts present as a net assessment to China’s rulers?
  5. To what extent is the Cold War a useful analogy for thinking about the rivalry between the United States and China?
  6. What will it take to deter war with China?
  7. What strategic guidance can American and Chinese political and military leaders derive from studying Mahan?
  8. Are the strategic theories of Mahan and Corbett still applicable?
  9. Can the United States retain command of the commons in the face of China’s growing operational capabilities to contest the maritime domain?
  10. Do nuclear and cyber weapons, artificial intelligence and machine learning make war between great powers more or less likely? Do these capabilities enhance deterrence or increase the prospects for escalation?
  11. In what ways can military planners integrate land, naval, air, nuclear, space, and cyber capabilities to achieve maximum strategic effectiveness?
  12. What limitations will constrain the optimal integration of land, naval, air, nuclear, space, and cyber operations?
  13. What guidance can the strategic theorists examined in the Strategy and Policy Course offer for understanding conflict in the cyber domain?
  14. What role will nuclear weapons play in a conflict involving China and the United States? Can leaders avoid escalation that leads to their use, or is it likely that they will be employed once fighting starts?
  15. China and the United States fought each other during the Korean War. What strategic insights does that conflict hold for American planners preparing for war with China? What lessons might Chinese leaders draw from the study of this conflict against the United States?
  16. What role will America’s allies play in meeting the challenge posed by China?
  17. How has Russia’s war on Ukraine and the United States months-long war with Iran affected China’s foreign-policy ambitions and strategic calculations?
  18. How can the United States, as a global power, best balance its capabilities between Russia’s threat to European security, Iran’s threat to the Middle East, and the strategic challenge posed by China in Asia?
  19. Some argue that the United States could stumble into a war with China without first considering the costs, risks, and likely course of a conflict. Could U.S. leaders commit to war without a clear idea of what they want to achieve and what strategy to follow?
  20. How might a war between China and the United States end?
