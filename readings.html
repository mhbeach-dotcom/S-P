Case I: Theories of War Nov 13–19

  1. Clausewitz, Carl von. On War. Michael Howard and Peter Paret, eds. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1976. BOOK Please note, On War is divided into eight books, each of the eight books is subdivided into chapters. The following are the assigned readings: • The Front Matter to On War: Preface by Marie von Clausewitz and Two Notes by the Author (pages 65-71). • Book One: All Chapters. (pp. 75-123) • Book Two: Chapters 1-3 and 5-6. (pp. 127-150, 156-174) • Book Three: Chapters 1-8, 16. (pp. 177-197, 216-219) • Book Four: Chapter 11. (pp. 258-262) • Book Six: Chapters 1, 5, 6, 26, and 27. (pp. 357-359, 370-376, 479-487) • • Book Seven: Chapters 2, 4, 5, and 22. (pp. 524-525, 527-528, 566-573) Book Eight: All Chapters. (pp. 577-633)
    This translation of On War, by historians Howard and Paret with commentary by strategic analyst Bernard Brodie, was much heralded when it appeared in 1976, in the immediate aftermath of the United States’ involvement in the Vietnam War. It remains the most widely read English-language version of Clausewitz’s work.
  2. Sun Tzu. The Art of War. Samuel B. Griffith, trans. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1980. Pages 57-149. BOOK
    Griffith’s experience in the United States Marine Corps, as well as his deep knowledge of Asian languages and cultures, makes his translation of Sun Tzu both scholarly and approachable for the professional military officer.
  3. Liddell Hart, Sir B. H. Strategy. New York: Meridian, second revised edition, 1991. Pages 319-323, 353-360. Leganto
    Liddell Hart, one of the most prolific and important British writers on strategic affairs in the twentieth century, introduces the concept of “grand strategy.” This passage also supplies an important definition of “victory,” and thoughts on the transitory nature of war termination.
  4. Mahan, Alfred Thayer. Mahan on Naval Strategy Selections from the Writings of Rear Admiral Alfred Thayer Mahan, John B. Hattendorf, ed. Annapolis: Naval Institute Press, 1991. Pages 27-96. BOOK
    This reading introduces some of the major themes in Mahan’s writings: sea power, maritime strategy, and geopolitics exerted a major influence on statesmen and naval planners in the era of the First World War. This reading examines Mahan’s six elements of sea power and links them to principles of naval strategy.
  5. Maurer, John H. “Alfred Thayer Mahan and the Strategy of Sea Power” in the New Makers of Modern Strategy, Hal Brands ed. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2023. Pages 169-192. BOOK
    Maurer, a Naval War College Distinguished University Professor, examines Mahan on sea power, maritime strategy, naval warfare, and great power competitions.

Case II: Peloponnesian War Nov 20 – Dec 2

  1. Strassler, Robert B., ed. The Landmark Thucydides. New York: The Free Press, 1996. Books I-VIII, Pages 3-548. Books 1-8. BOOK
    Thucydides covers all eleven Strategy and Policy course themes in his account of this war, compelling his readers to think through the interrelationship of policy, strategy, and operations and the integration and application of naval power.] Key passages: Book I – The origins of the conflict, pages 3-85. With particular emphasis on the speeches, the Persian Wars and the Delian League, pages 49-54, and the Athenian-Spartan conflict from 460-446 B.C., pages 56-63. Book II – Outbreak of the war, pages 89-107. – Pericles’ Funeral Oration, the plague in Athens, and the policy of Pericles, pages 110-128. Book III – The revolt of Mytilene, pages 159-167. – The Mytilenean debate, pages 175-184. – The Plataean debate, pages 185-193. – The Corcyrean civil war, pages 194-201. Book IV – Athens’ success at Pylos, pages 223-246. – Brasidas in Thrace, pages 263-272. – Brasidas captures Amphipolis, pages 279-285. Book V – Peace of Nicias, pages 309-316. – Alliance between Athens and Argos and the Battle of Mantinea, pages 327-350. – The Melian Dialogue, pages 350-357. Book VI – Launching of the Sicilian expedition, pages 361-379. Book VII – Athenian disaster in Sicily, pages 427-478. Book VIII – Reaction to Athenian defeat in Sicily, pages 481-483. – Spartan treaties with Persia, pages 492, 501-502, and 514-515. – Alcibiades advises Persia, pages 506-508. – The Athenian coup, pages 508-512, 517-525, and 532-540.
  2. Kagan, Donald. The Peloponnesian War. New York: Penguin, 2004. Pages 1-54. Leganto
    Kagan’s account is helpful for understanding the events leading to the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War as well as the geopolitical context and coalition dynamics of fifth century B.C. Greece.
  3. Roberts, Jennifer T. The Plague of War: Athens, Sparta, and the Struggle for Ancient Greece. London: Oxford, 2017. Pages 237-294. BOOK
    This selection from Roberts picks up the narrative of the war where Thucydides leaves off and carries through the Ionian War, including the crucial naval battles of Arginusae and Aegospotami, ending with the deposition of democracy in Athens.
  4. Lebow, Richard Ned. “Thucydides and Deterrence.” Security Studies, 16 (2007). Pages 163-188. Leganto
    This article by former Strategy and Policy visiting professor Ned Lebow examines deterrence and compellence failures as possible explanations for the origins and course of the Peloponnesian War.
  5. Ulrich, Marybeth. “An International Relations Primer for Strategy and Policy.” Selected Readings
    This reading by the Chair of the Strategy and Policy Department offers a primer on modern international relations (IR) theories that the students may find useful to understand Thucydides’ account of the Peloponnesian War and in subsequent case studies.

Case III: FrRev & Napoleon Dec 8–11

  1. Kennedy, Paul. The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers: Economic Change and Military Conflict from 1500 to 2000. New York: Random House, 1987. Pages 115-139. BOOK
    Kennedy provides a grand strategic overview of the period addressed by this case study. He describes the European balance of power in the eighteenth century, emphasizing financial developments and geopolitical trends. In addition, Kennedy provides a synopsis of the period from the end of the Seven Years’ War in 1763 until the downfall of Napoleon in 1815, focusing on Britain and France and their position in the European state system.
  2. Doyle, William. The French Revolution: A Very Short Introduction. New York: Oxford University Press, 2001. Pages 19-64. BOOK
    Doyle provides a brief overview of the French Revolution and explains its significance.
  3. Weigley, Russell F. The Age of Battles: The Quest for Decisive Warfare from Breitenfeld to Waterloo. Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1991. Pages 279-543. BOOK
    Whereas reading no. 1 (Kennedy’s Rise and Fall of the Great Powers) addresses the case from the grand strategic level, Weigley provides an overview of warfare during the French Revolution and Napoleonic Era at the levels of strategy and operations. Weigley critiques the leadership of Napoleon, Wellington, and a host of other senior military officers while placing these leaders in the context of an evolving profession of arms. The reading also serves as a point of departure for assessing the potential decisiveness of the military instrument to the exclusion of the other instruments of national power.
  4. Duffy, Michael. “British Policy in the War against Revolutionary France,” in Britain and Revolutionary France: Conflict, Subversion and Propaganda, Colin James, ed. Exeter Studies in History, no. 5. Exeter: University of Exeter, 1983. Pages 11-26. Leganto
    Duffy identifies four main British policies during the French Revolution and Napoleonic era. He then explains how the British implemented these policies to develop a policy-strategy match.
  5. Corbett, Julian S. Some Principles of Maritime Strategy. Annapolis: Naval Institute Press Edition, 1988. Part I: Chapters 1-5; Part II: Chapters 1 & 3; Appendix: the “Green Pamphlet” War Course: Notes on Strategy. BOOK
    Sir Julian Corbett admired and built upon Clausewitz’s On War, adapting it to offer strategic guidance for maritime powers. His Some Principles of Maritime Strategy is important for understanding Britain’s strategy in the Napoleonic Wars.
  6. Corbett, Julian S. “Napoleon and the British Navy after Trafalgar.” The Quarterly Review, vol. 237, no 471 (April 1922). Pages 238-255. Selected Readings
    This article addresses Trafalgar and British decision-making in its aftermath. Of particular importance is Corbett’s concept of the “disposal force” or the use of a land force for the purpose of expeditionary warfare.
  7. Mahan, Alfred Thayer. The Influence of Sea Power upon the French Revolution and Empire 1793-1812. Vol. 2. 9th edition. Boston: Little, Brown, 1898. Pages 375-411. Selected Readings
    Alfred Thayer Mahan is renowned for his writings on sea power, maritime strategy, naval warfare, and international relations. In this excerpt from The Influence of Sea Power upon the French Revolution and Empire, Mahan examines the effectiveness of sea powers in long-term competitions and their means of defeating continental powers.
  8. Davey, James. In Nelson’s Wake: The Navy and the Napoleonic Wars. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2015. Pages 230-253. Leganto
    This chapter provides an overview of economic warfare in the maritime domain. This includes privateers, blockades, convoys, and most importantly, Napoleon’s Continental System.
  9. Ross, Steve. “Caging the Eagle: Napoleonic War Coalitions,” in Naval Coalition Warfare: From the Napoleonic War to Operation Iraqi Freedom, Bruce A. Elleman and S.C.M. Paine, eds. London: Routledge, 2008. Pages 25-32. Leganto
    Ross, a former Naval War College professor, examines coalition dynamics to assess Britain’s evolving role and explains the success of the final coalition in defeating Napoleon.
  10. Kissinger, Henry. Diplomacy. New York: Simon and Schuster, 1994. Pages 78-102. BOOK
    Kissinger highlights the events and personalities surrounding the Congress of Vienna and the Concert of Europe that emerged in the aftermath of Napoleon’s defeat. He emphasizes strategic leadership in shaping the international environment as Europe transitioned from decades of war to almost a century without a major European-wide war.

Case IV: Rise of Germany & WWI Dec 15–18

  1. Kennedy, Paul. The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers: Economic Change and Military Conflict from 1500 to 2000. New York: Random House, 1987. p.182-203 and 215-274. BOOK
    This overview reading presents the rise and fall of Imperial Germany in an international context. The first part discusses the political, military, economic, and technological conditions underlying the Prussian-German successes in the 1864-1871 Unification Wars. It then bridges to the challenges Imperial Germany presented for its neighbors, followed by a review of the international order in the late nineteenth century. The second part examines how the rest of the great powers, including the United States, developed economically and politically from the late nineteenth century to 1914. It then offers a grand strategic overview of the First World War.
  2. Clark, Christopher. Iron Kingdom: The Rise and Downfall of Prussia, 1600–1947. London: Penguin Books, 2006. 510-555. Leganto
    Christopher Clark, a leading expert in German history, offers a comprehensive overview of the Wars of German Unification, their course, and the international and domestic context. It also sheds light on Bismarck’s role and the challenges the Prussian military faced.
  3. Murray, Michelle. “Weltpolitik: The German Aspiration for World Power Status.” The Struggle for Recognition in International Relations: Status, Revisionism, and Rising Power, p. 87-112. Selected Readings
    Murray offers novel analysis of the rationale behind Weltpolitik and the naval build-up. She discusses the Navy’s role as an instrument of nation-building and international policy and suggests possible off-ramps to lower European tensions in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The reading bridges the period between German Unification and 1914.
  4. MacMillan, Margaret. “Strategy, War Plans, and the First World War,” in The New Makers of Modern Strategy, p.471-494. BOOK.
    The chapter offers a comprehensive and accessible review of the challenges faced by the major players on the eve of the First World War and how they sought to solve them. MacMillan also provides insight into civil-military relations in Europe during this period, a major theme of the case study. She also discusses the strengths and weaknesses of the pre-1914 coalitions.
  5. Strachan, Hew. The First World War. New York: Viking, 2004. Chapters 2, 4-10. Pages 3-64, 97-340. BOOK
    Hew Strachan, one of the world’s leading authorities on the First World War, presents a lucid account of this hideous conflict, providing essential background information for evaluating Germany’s policy and strategy.
  6. Kennedy, Paul. The Rise and Fall of British Naval Mastery. London: Ashfield Press, paperback edition, 1987. Chapters 8-9. Pages 205-265. BOOK
    These chapters examine Great Britain’s response to the growing threats it faced in the maritime environment at the beginning of the twentieth century. Kennedy appraises Britain’s efforts to stay ahead of the challenge posed by the German naval buildup engineered by Tirpitz. The reading then provides an overview of the British naval strategy in the First World War.
  7. Offer, Avner. The First World War: An Agrarian Interpretation. Oxford: Clarendon Press, paperback edition, 1991. Chapters 22-24. Pages 321-367. Selected Readings
    This insightful reading explains how economic, political, and military factors intertwine in the German strategic choices and pays special attention to the navy’s role in these processes. The first chapter discusses the Tirpitz Plan and economic and social forces driving Imperial Germany’s naval buildup in the late nineteenth century. The second examines economic and social pressure shaping the German desire for a short war on the eve of 1914. The last chapter explores the flawed assessments and planning assumptions behind Germany’s decision to embark on a disruptive, asymmetric strategy of unrestricted submarine warfare.
  8. Kagan, Robert. “Woodrow Wilson and the Rise of American Grand Strategy,” in The New Makers of Modern Strategy, Hal Brands, ed. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2023. Pages 545-568. BOOK
    This reading analyzes the difficult path toward war termination. Kagan examines the rise of the United States within the international system and President Woodrow Wilson’s views on American power and purpose on the world stage.

Case V: Interwar Jan 5–8

  1. Kagan, Donald. On the Origins of War and the Preservation of Peace. New York: Doubleday, 1995. Pages 281-417. BOOK
    Kagan provides a general overview of the key issues from the end of fighting in the First World War to the reemergence of global war in 1939.
  2. Kennedy, Paul. The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers: Economic Change and Military Conflict from 1500 to 2000. New York: Random House, 1987. Pages 275-343. BOOK
    Kennedy explores the relationship between a country’s international position and its economic vitality. The assigned chapter examines the period between the two world wars.
  3. Marks, Sally. “Mistakes and Myths: The Allies, Germany and the Versailles Treaty, 1918-1921.” The Journal of Modern History, vol. 85, no. 3 (September 2013). Pages 632-659. Selected Readings
    Marks emphasizes that the current scholarly consensus is that the Treaty of Versailles was not fatally flawed, did not cause the Depression, the rise of Hitler, or the Second World War. Yet many readers cling to these ideas. Marks considers how an intense propaganda campaign launched in Germany and Britain against the treaty and an unfavorable international situation made enforcement of the treaty difficult and contributed to the undermining of the postwar peace even before Hitler took power in Germany.
  4. Gooch, John. “‘Building buffers and filling vacuums’: Great Britain and the Middle East, 1914-1922” in The Making of Peace: Rulers, States, and the Aftermath of War, Williamson Murray and Jim Lacey, eds. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2009. Pages 240-264. Leganto
    This essay provides background on Britain’s foreign policy choices in the Middle East.
  5. Townshend, Charles. “Civilization and ‘Frightfulness’: Air Control in the Middle East between the Wars,” in Warfare, Diplomacy and Politics: Essays in Honour of A. J. P. Taylor, Chris Wrigley, ed. London: Hamish Hamilton, 1986. Pages 142-162. Leganto
    This article explores the limitations of air power as instrument of British imperial control in the Middle East as a cheaper alternative to using large numbers of ground forces.
  6. Paine, S. C. M. The Wars for Asia, 1911-1949. New York: Cambridge University Press, 2012. Pages 3-70. BOOK
    Paine, a Strategy & Policy and NWC University Professor Emerita, traces the collapse of East Asian regional stability. Successive chapters cover Japan’s territorial ambitions on the Asian mainland and strategic competition with Russia and China, as well as China’s struggles to maintain its national unity amidst social unrest while surrounded by revisionist powers.
  7. Bell, P.M.H. The Origins of the Second World War in Europe. third edition, New York: Pearson Longman, 2007. Pages 60-184. E-book/Leganto
    Bell analyzes the underlying ideological and political forces at work in Europe on the eve World War II to include Italian Fascism, German Nazism, Soviet Communism a parliamentary democracy in France and Britain. Bell also analyzes the Great Depression’s effects on international relations and traces the economic factors that contributed to the outbreak of war.
  8. Murray, Williamson and Allan R. Millett, eds. Military Innovation in the Interwar Period. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996. Pages 329-383. BOOK
    This study examines how the armed forces of the major powers during the interwar period developed the doctrine, force structure, and weapons that they would employ during the Second World War. Studying military transformation from a comparative perspective provides insight into how the British armed services fell behind those of competitors between the wars.
  9. Kennedy, Paul. The Rise and Fall of British Naval Mastery. Atlantic Heights, New Jersey: The Ashfield Press, 1987. Pages 267-298. BOOK
    Kennedy examines Britain’s challenges in maintaining its position of naval leadership between the two world wars
  10. Maiolo, Joseph A. “Did the Royal Navy Decline Between the Two World Wars.” The RUSI Journal, vol. 159, no. 4 (2014). Pages 18-24. Leganto
    Maiolo argues that Britain’s navy remained strong relative to its international challengers.
  11. Murray, Williamson. “Munich, 1938: The Military Confrontation.” The Journal of Strategic Studies, vol. 2, no. 3 (December 1979). Pages 282-302. Leganto
    Murray assesses pre-WWII European power dynamics and analyzes whether Britain and France should have fought in 1938 rather than 1939.

Case VI: WWII Jan 12–15

  1. Mawdsley, Evan. World War II: A New History. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, second edition, 2020. Pages 1- 364. (BOOK)
    One of the world’s leading historians of the Second World War provides an insightful up-todate account of the global conflict. His assessment of the political aims, strategic choices, and conduct of operations offers essential background for undertaking a policy and strategy analysis of the war.
  2. Simms, Brendan. “Strategies of Geopolitical Revolution: Hitler and Stalin,” in Hal Brands, ed., The New Makers of Modern Strategy: From the Ancient World to the Digital Age (Princeton and Oxford: Princeton University Press, 2023), pp. 616-637. (BOOK)
    Simms compares the grand strategies of Hitler and Stalin and evaluates the ideological, geopolitical, and pragmatic considerations that shaped them.
  3. Wegner, Bernd. “The Road to Defeat: The German Campaigns in Russia 1941-43.” Journal of Strategic Studies, vol. 13, no. 1 (1990). Pages 105-127. Leganto
    Wegner addresses the first years of the war on the Eastern Front from the German perspective, showing how German strategic choices in the war against the Soviet Union contributed to Germany’s eventual defeat. Wegner also details the role of Nazi ideology in decision-making by Hitler and his generals.
  4. Baer, George W. One Hundred Years of Sea Power: The U.S. Navy, 1890-1990. Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1994. Pages 146-272. (BOOK)
    In this prize-winning book, George Baer provides an important account of the rise of the United States Navy to global leadership, capping a fifty-year great-power contest for naval mastery. His study examines the buildup of the Navy on the eve of war and its role in bringing about the defeat of Nazi Germany and imperial Japan. This reading is especially valuable for strategic assessments of the global management of American forces during the war.
  5. Plan Dog Memorandum (November 12, 1940). Selected Readings
    The Plan Dog memorandum, drafted by Chief of Naval Operations (and Naval War College graduate) H.R. Stark, assessed a possible two-front war in Europe and the Pacific. Stark explores options and recommends a policy of prioritizing Europe while holding in the Pacific.
  6. Biddle, Tami Davis. “Democratic Leaders and Strategies of Coalition Warfare: Churchill and Roosevelt in World War II,” in Hal Brands, ed., The New Makers of Modern Strategy: From the Ancient World to the Digital Age (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2023), pp. 569-592. (BOOK)
    Biddle explores key differences between totalitarian and democratic states and examines AngloAmerican strategic debates as well as disagreements over war termination and the desired political end-state of the war in Europe.
  7. O’Brien, Phillips. “East versus West in the Defeat of Nazi Germany.” Journal of Strategic Studies. vol. 23, no. 2 (June 2000). Pages 89-113. Leganto
    O’Brien challenges the view that Soviet ground forces were largely responsible for the defeat of Nazi Germany. He highlights the importance of American Lend-Lease aid to the Red Army and the powerful strategic effects of the Anglo-American bombing of the German homeland.
  8. Harrison, Mark. “The Soviet Union: The Defeated Victor,” in Mark Harrison, ed., The Economics of World War II: Six Great Powers in International Comparison. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1998), pp. 268-301. Leganto
    This study examines the mobilization of Soviet resources for the war effort and the contribution of the Soviet Union to the success of the Grand Alliance.

Case VII: Early Cold War Jan 19–27

  1. Westad, Odd Arne. The Cold War: A World History. New York: Basic Books, 2017. Pages 19-259. BOOK
    Westad reframes the Cold War not simply as a U.S.–Soviet rivalry but as a global ideological struggle rooted in the transformations of the early 20th century. For strategy students, these chapters emphasize how strategic choices are shaped by ideological commitments and historical contexts beyond traditional military calculations.
  2. Kennedy, Paul. The Rise and Fall of Great Powers. New York: Vintage Books, 1989. Pages 347-372. BOOK
    Kennedy explains how the United States and the Soviet Union emerged from World War II as the two dominant global powers and argues that their conflicting political systems and strategic goals made the Cold War a near certainty.
  3. Leffler, Melvyn. “The emergence of an American grand strategy, 1945-1952.” In The Cambridge History of the Cold War, Volume 1, edited by Melvyn Leffler and Odd Arne Westad. Pages 67-89. Selected Readings
    Leffler argues that U.S. Cold War grand strategy was shaped more by systemic insecurity and political ideology than by reactive containment. The article stresses the integration of economic, military, and ideological tools into a holistic grand strategy, challenging students to consider how long-term planning and institutional momentum shape strategic outcomes.
  4. Pechatnov, Vladimir O. “The Soviet Union and the World, 1944-1953.” In The Cambridge History of the Cold War, Volume 1, edited by Melvyn Leffler and Odd Arne Westad. Pages 90-111. Selected Readings
    Pechatnov presents an internal Soviet view of Cold War diplomacy, focusing on the Kremlin’s efforts to navigate both ideological imperatives and geopolitical constraints. The reading highlights the complex interplay between domestic pressures and strategic behavior in authoritarian regimes.
  5. Hitchcock, William. “The Marshall Plan and the Creation of the West.” In The Cambridge History of the Cold War, Volume 1, edited by Melvyn Leffler and Odd Arne Westad. Pages 154-174. Selected Readings
    Hitchcock's analysis of the Marshall Plan presents it as both an economic recovery initiative and a bold geopolitical strategy. The article unpacks tensions between strategic generosity and conditional sovereignty, showing how aid served as an instrument of containment.
  6. Toprani, Anand. “‘Our Efforts Have Degenerated into a Competition for Dollars’. The ‘Revolt of the Admirals’, NSC-68, and the Political Economy of the Cold War." Diplomacy & Statecraft 30, no. 4 (2020). Pages 681-706. Leganto
    Toprani, a professor in the Strategy & Policy department, shows how resource constraints and inter-service rivalry eroded strategic coherence, turning high-level planning into a competition for appropriations. This reading highlights the material limits of grand strategy and reveals how domestic politics and institutional interests shape strategic execution.
  7. Wells, Samuel F., Jr. "Sounding the Tocsin: NSC 68 and the Soviet Threat." International Security 4, no. 2 (Fall 1979). Pages 116-158. Leganto
    Wells analyzes NSC-68 as both a strategic document and a rhetorical call to arms, exploring how assumptions about Soviet intentions influenced U.S. capabilities, planning, and alliance formation. This reading challenges students to critically assess how threat perception and strategic imagination shape defense planning.
  8. Kaplan, Fred. The Bomb: Presidents, Generals, and the Secret History of Nuclear War. New York: Simon & Schuster, 2020. Pages 4-30. BOOK
    Kaplan describes how the U.S. developed its first plans for nuclear war in the early stages of the Cold War, demonstrating that early nuclear strategy was driven by a mix of fear, uncertainty, and the belief that a large-scale strike could deter Soviet aggression. This chapter highlights the lack of civilian control and planning in early nuclear decision-making, which set the stage for conflicts between military and civilian leaders over how to manage nuclear weapons.
  9. Rosenberg, David Alan. “American Atomic Strategy and the Hydrogen Bomb Decision.” The Journal of American History 66, no. 1 (1979). Pages 62–87. Leganto
    Rosenberg examines the internal debates within the U.S. government that led to the decision to develop the hydrogen bomb. It underscores the strategic dilemma posed by Soviet nuclear advances and raises questions about deterrence theory, escalation control, and the costs of technological overreach.
  10. Chen, Jian. Mao’s China and the Cold War. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 2001. Pages 49-117. BOOK
    Chen provides students with the Chinese perspective on the Cold War and the evolution of the PRC’s policy goals and strategies, emphasizing the importance of ideology and domestic politics.
  11. House, J. M. (2012). A Military History of the Cold War, 1944–1962, University of Oklahoma Press. Pages 133-208. BOOK
    These two chapters, on the Chinese Civil War and the Korean War, provide students with the narrative of operations and events that are important in this case.
  12. X
    George F. Kennan]. (1947). “The Sources of Soviet Conduct.” Foreign Affairs, vol. 25, no 4. Pages 566–582. (Selected Readings) [This article by a Foreign Service Officer and Russia expert had a remarkable impact on U.S. policy and strategy in the emerging Cold War. Kennan provided an influential assessment of the Soviet Union, the key concept of “containment” for thwarting Soviet strategy, and a “theory of victory” for bringing about the mellowing or breakup of the Soviet system.
  13. Primary Source Documents: Soviet Assessments of the United States in the Early Cold War. Selected Readings
    The Novikov telegram can be read as the Soviet counterpart to Kennan’s “X” article. It was drafted by the Soviet ambassador in Washington after Kennan wrote his long telegram but before the “X” article was published and depicts a United States intent on world domination. The Zhdanov report to the Communist Information Bureau, the successor organization to the Communist International, emphasizes the ideological differences between the United States and the Soviet Union and the imperative of containing U.S. imperialism.
  14. “NSC-68: A Report to the National Security Council.” Naval War College Review, vol. 27 (May-June 1975). Pages 51-108. Selected Readings
    NSC-68 was drafted in response to President Truman’s request for advice regarding nuclear policy in view of the likelihood that the Soviet Union had successfully tested an atomic weapon.

Case VIII: Middle Cold War Jan 28 – Feb 3

  1. Westad, Odd Arne. The Cold War: A World History. New York: Basic Books, 2017. Pages 261-363. BOOK
    Westad provides an overview of the time period of this case study, focusing on developments in the Third World within the overarching context of US and Soviet abiding interests in Europe.
  2. Seeing Red: The Development of Maoist Thought on Insurgency. Readings #7-#10. Selected Readings
    Bradford Lee, Professor Emeritus in the Strategy and Policy Department, selected these extracts from Mao’s writings on political revolution and irregular warfare, including his famous On Protracted War. Articles #7-#10 are particularly important in conveying Mao’s theory of revolution.
  3. Asselin, Pierre. Vietnam’s American War. New York: Cambridge University Press, 2018. Pages 47-233. BOOK
    Asselin gives the North Vietnamese communist perspective on the war in Indochina, enhancing student comprehension of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam’s policy objectives and strategies.
  4. Chen, Jian. Mao’s China and the Cold War. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 2001. Pages 118-237. BOOK
    Chen provides students with the Chinese perspective on the Cold War and the evolution of the PRC’s policy goals and strategies, emphasizing the importance of ideology and domestic politics.
  5. Kaplan, Fred. The Bomb: Presidents, Generals, and the Secret History of Nuclear War. New York: Simon and Schuster, 2020. Pages 31-120. BOOK
    This reading sheds light on the institutional dimension of strategy, especially civ/mil relations, and the instruments of national power in the nuclear age.
  6. House, Jonathan M. A Military History of the Cold War, 1944-1962. Norman: University of Oklahoma, Press, 2012. Pages 415-440. BOOK
    House provides a succinct overview of the Cuban Missile Crisis, which prepares students for the historical role-playing simulation.
  7. Ulrich, Marybeth P. "Civil-Military Relations for Strategy and Policy," Strategy and Policy Department Primer Series, January 2026. Selected Readings
    Professor Ulrich examines how interaction among civilian authorities, military professionals, and society shape the formulation and execution of strategy, influence strategic adaptability, and condition political support in war.

Case IX: Late Cold War Feb 4–9

  1. Westad, Odd Arne. The Cold War: A World History. New York: Basic Books, 2017. Pages 365-629. BOOK
    This core reading provides a broad history of the final decades of the Cold War, analyzing the ideological, strategic, and geopolitical factors that influenced strategy and policy by the two superpowers, their allies, and non-aligned actors. Chapters 14-22 are particularly pertinent for understanding how regional conflicts and global power dynamics shaped strategic thinking in the late Cold War period. The conclusion offers some key takeaways that pertain to understanding the contemporary strategic environment and inform the formulation and implementation of national strategy within it.
  2. Kaplan, Fred. The Bomb: Presidents, Generals, and the Secret History of Nuclear War. New York: Simon & Schuster, 2020. Pages 100–175. BOOK
    Kaplan investigates the development of nuclear strategy during the late Cold War. The reading focuses on presidential decision-making and debates within the U.S. military. The reader will gain insight into nuclear weapons’ influence on strategic options and deterrence.
  3. Brands, Hal. Making the Unipolar Moment, U.S. Foreign Policy and the Rise of the Post-Cold War Order. Ithaca and London: Cornell University Press, 2016. (Pages 14-39, 53-79, 85-97, 114-118.) BOOK
    Brands explores the emergence of American primacy after the Cold War. This reading examines the implications of policy choices while terminating a cold war and transitioning between strategic eras.
  4. Jonathan House. A Military History of the Cold War, 1962-1991. Norman: University of Oklahoma Press, 2020. Pages 227-371. E-book/Leganto
    House presents a study of the relationship between policy and military force during a time of global upheaval and political alignment, providing a perspective on how the realities of time, distance, resources, and military culture often constrained and diverted the inclinations or policies of world leaders.
  5. Gaddis, John Lewis. "Hanging Tough Paid Off." Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists 45, no. 6 (1989). Pages 18–23. Leganto
